Pop art is an art movement that took its images from popular and commercial culture: comic strips, adverts, product packaging, Hollywood films, pop music and celebrities. It emerged in Britain in the mid-1950s and in America in the late 1950s, and it peaked in the 1960s. Its best-known works, like Andy Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans and Roy Lichtenstein's comic-strip paintings, put everyday mass-produced pictures into the art gallery.

What is pop art in simple words?

Pop art is art about the pictures everyone already knows. Instead of landscapes or abstract color, pop artists painted soup cans, comic panels, film stars and road signs. Britannica says the movement had no single style or attitude; it was a varied response to the postwar world of shopping and mass media.

MoMA's definition adds why they did it: pop artists borrowed images from television, comic books and print advertising, often to challenge the values the mass media spread, from ideas about femininity and home life to consumerism and patriotism. They also borrowed methods from the commercial world, such as printing.

So a pop art work usually does two things at once. It enjoys the bright, cheap, catchy image, and it makes you notice how strange it is to see that image enlarged on a museum wall.

When and where did pop art start?

Pop art started in Britain. Britannica says a group of artists, architects and design historians met regularly at the Institute of Contemporary Arts in London in 1952–55 to talk about things like car styling and pulp magazines. They called themselves the Independent Group, and Tate calls them the precursor to British pop art.

Two members mattered most. Eduardo Paolozzi lectured to the group in 1952 on his collection of American science fiction and pulp imagery. Richard Hamilton made the collage Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing? in 1956, and in a 1957 letter to the architects Peter and Alison Smithson he listed what pop art should be: popular, transient, expendable, low cost, mass produced, young, witty, sexy, gimmicky, glamorous and big business.

In America the movement took off in the late 1950s. Britannica names Jasper Johns, Larry Rivers and Robert Rauschenberg as the immediate predecessors, painting flags and beer cans in the 1950s. Further back stood Dada and Marcel Duchamp, who had celebrated mass-produced objects decades earlier.

What does pop art look like?

Pop art looks like print. Tate describes American pop as a return to recognizable images, with hard edges and distinct forms after the loose brushwork of Abstract Expressionism. Its sources were Hollywood movies, advertising, packaging, pop music and comic books.

Signs that a picture is pop art:

  1. The subject is a brand, a product, a celebrity or a comic panel.
  2. Outlines are hard and colors are flat, like a poster or a printed page.
  3. The same image is repeated, as in Warhol's rows of soup cans.
  4. Commercial techniques show through: screenprinting, enlarged printing dots, collage from magazines.
  5. The tone is cool or ironic rather than personal.

Roy Lichtenstein is the clearest case of the comic-book look. His painting Whaam! (1963), now at Tate Modern, shows a fighter plane firing a rocket that hits another plane in the second panel. He adapted it from several comic-book panels, chiefly one drawn by Irv Novick for a 1962 war comic, and changed it by splitting it into a two-panel diptych.

Who are the most famous pop artists?

Andy Warhol and Roy Lichtenstein are the most famous, but the movement was much wider. MoMA's collection of pop art includes Claes Oldenburg, James Rosenquist, Tom Wesselmann, Marisol, Rosalyn Drexler and Yayoi Kusama as well as Warhol, Lichtenstein and Richard Hamilton.

Warhol started as a commercial artist in New York. His best-known early silkscreen paintings include Campbell's Soup Cans (1961–62), Marilyn Diptych (1962) and Coca-Cola (3) (1962). MoMA holds both the Soup Cans of 1962 and Gold Marilyn Monroe.

In Britain, a second generation came out of the Royal College of Art in the early 1960s: Peter Blake, Pauline Boty, Richard Smith and Joe Tilson, followed by David Hockney, Patrick Caulfield and R.B. Kitaj. Blake helped design the cover of the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967), one of the best-known images of British pop.

How was British pop art different from American pop art?

British pop looked at American culture from outside; American pop lived inside it. Tate says early British pop was fueled by American popular culture seen from a distance, while American artists drew on what they saw and lived every day.

The tone differed too. Tate describes the British side as more academic, using irony and parody and focusing on what American imagery stood for and how it shaped people's lives. The American side was a direct return to recognizable images after Abstract Expressionism, with impersonal, ordinary subjects and little personal symbolism. Britannica adds that Pauline Boty used photo-based works to question how magazines objectified women.

How did pop art reach film, TV and animation?

Pop art borrowed from the screen and gave back to it. Hollywood films were one of its main sources, and its artists worked with moving images themselves. Between 1963 and 1968 Andy Warhol made more than 600 underground films, including silent close-up Screen Tests and Empire, an eight-hour static shot of the Empire State Building.

Television took the look straight back. When the producer William Dozier read Batman comics to prepare the 1966 Batman TV series, he concluded that the only way to make it work was as a pop-art camp comedy. Warhol had already made an unauthorized Batman homage, Batman Dracula, in 1964.

Animation keeps returning to the printing marks that pop artists enlarged. Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse (2018) used halftones and Ben-Day dots to make colors and shading, misaligned colors as if badly printed, and sound words written into the image, all to make a computer-animated film look like a comic book. Is pop art still around today?

Pop art as a movement belongs to the 1950s and 1960s, but its methods stayed. Tate calls it one of the first signs of postmodernism and notes that other countries added their own versions in the 1960s and 70s. In China in the 1980s, political pop combined Western pop art with socialist realism.

Its habits are now everywhere: brand logos as subject matter, repeated portraits, flat color and comic dots in posters, album covers and title sequences. For neighbors on the timeline, compare it with the abstract art it reacted against and the Art Deco graphics of an earlier mass-produced age. Questions people ask

What is pop art in simple words?

It is art made from the images of everyday mass culture: comic strips, adverts, supermarket products, film stars and pop music. Pop artists took these 'low' pictures and put them in galleries, often enlarged, repeated or slightly changed, to make people look again at the images they saw every day.

Who started pop art?

It started in Britain with the Independent Group, artists, architects and critics who met at London's Institute of Contemporary Arts in 1952–55. Richard Hamilton and Eduardo Paolozzi were key members. In America, Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein and others took it up in the late 1950s and early 1960s.

What are the main characteristics of pop art?

Recognizable images from popular culture, such as comics, brands and celebrities; hard edges and distinct forms; flat, bright color; repetition; and methods borrowed from commercial printing, like screenprinting. In 1957 Richard Hamilton listed pop art as popular, transient, expendable, low cost, mass produced, young, witty, sexy, gimmicky, glamorous and big business.

What is the most famous pop art work?

Andy Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans (1962), now at MoMA, is the usual answer. Other famous works include Roy Lichtenstein's Whaam! (1963) at Tate Modern, Warhol's Gold Marilyn Monroe (1962) and Peter Blake's cover for the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967).

How is pop art different from Abstract Expressionism?

They are opposites. Abstract Expressionism, which came just before, was loose, gestural and personal. Tate says American pop art returned to recognizable images, hard edges and distinct forms, and used impersonal, everyday pictures to move away from personal feeling and symbolism.
