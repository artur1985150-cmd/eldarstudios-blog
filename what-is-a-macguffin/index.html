In short

  • A MacGuffin is an object, person or goal that drives the plot because the characters want it, while what it actually is hardly matters.
  • Screenwriter Angus MacPhail coined the word; Alfred Hitchcock made it famous and explained it with a joke about a lion trap.
  • Classic examples: the falcon statuette in The Maltese Falcon (1941), the briefcase in Pulp Fiction (1994), the secrets in North by Northwest (1959).
  • Hitchcock thought the audience should not care about the MacGuffin; George Lucas thought it should care almost as much as the characters.
  • A quick test: if you could swap the thing for something else and the story would barely change, it is a MacGuffin.

A MacGuffin is the object, person or goal that everyone in a story is chasing, even though what it actually is hardly matters. It sets the plot in motion and gives the characters a reason to fight, lie and run. The falcon statuette in The Maltese Falcon and the briefcase in Pulp Fiction are classic examples. Alfred Hitchcock made the word famous; he put it this way: the spies care about it, the audience doesn't.

What does MacGuffin mean?

Wikipedia defines it as something "necessary to the plot and the motivation of the characters, but insignificant, unimportant, or irrelevant in itself." It is a plot device: a tool the writer uses to get the story going.

Usually the MacGuffin shows up in the first act. After that it matters less and less. It may come back at the climax, and sometimes the story forgets it entirely by the end.

It does not have to be an object. StudioBinder counts a goal, a person or an idea, as long as the characters pursue it. What makes it a MacGuffin is that it only matters because it moves the plot forward. You will also see the spelling McGuffin; both are in use.

A note before the examples: the sections below name plot points of older films, though no endings are given away.

Where does the word MacGuffin come from?

English screenwriter Angus MacPhail coined it for film, and Hitchcock picked it up. Nobody knows for sure why that name. One suggestion quoted by Wikipedia is the word "guff," meaning something trivial or worthless.

Hitchcock explained it with a joke he told in a 1939 lecture at Columbia University in New York. Two men are on a train. One asks about a package in the luggage rack, and the other says it is a MacGuffin, a device for trapping lions in the Scottish Highlands. "But there are no lions in the Scottish Highlands," says the first. "Well then, that's no MacGuffin!" Hitchcock's point was that a MacGuffin is "actually nothing at all."

He told the same story in his 1966 interview with François Truffaut and on television. No Film School quotes his shortest version: it is "the thing that the characters on the screen worry about but the audience doesn't care about."

The idea is far older than the name. Wikipedia points to the Holy Grail of Arthurian legend and the Golden Fleece sought by Jason and the Argonauts. In the silent serials of the World War I era, actress Pearl White called the same thing a "weenie": a roll of film, a rare coin or a diamond that every hero and villain was after.

Hitchcock rated the North by Northwest secrets as his best MacGuffin, according to the Hitchcock site The MacGuffin, because they barely exist at all. The same page notes that the hero's middle initial, "O," stands for nothing either.

In Mel Brooks's Hitchcock parody High Anxiety (1977), the hero's hotel room is moved at the request of "a Mr. MacGuffin." A small joke for people who know the term.

Hitchcock vs George Lucas: should the audience care?

Here the two directors disagree. For Hitchcock, the MacGuffin was an excuse: the audience should watch the people, the suspense and the chase, never the object.

George Lucas, as Vanity Fair reported (quoted by Wikipedia), believes the audience should care about the MacGuffin almost as much as the heroes and villains do. He praised the Ark of the Covenant in Raiders of the Lost Ark as an excellent one and called the one in Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade "feeble."

No Film School splits examples into two groups along these lines. In the Hitchcock group are things that matter to characters but mean nothing to the story, like the stolen money in Psycho or the Heart of the Ocean necklace in Titanic. In the Lucas group are things the audience cares about too, such as the Ark, the ring in The Lord of the Rings and the Horcruxes in Harry Potter.

Some writers would not call the Lucas kind a MacGuffin at all. StudioBinder uses the term "plot coupon" for objects like the Ark and the Infinity Stones. Its argument: the Infinity Stones have rules, the audience cares about them, and they cannot be swapped without changing the ending. Wikipedia uses "plot coupons" differently, for several MacGuffins that characters collect to "trade in" for an ending. If you meet the term, check which meaning the writer has in mind.

MacGuffin vs red herring vs Chekhov's gun

These three get mixed up because each is "something in the story that turns out to matter, or not." They do different jobs.

Device What it is Does it pay off? Example
MacGuffin The thing characters want, unimportant in itself It drives the plot, but its nature barely matters The briefcase in Pulp Fiction
Red herring A false clue that misleads the audience No, by design; it points the wrong way The word "Rache" in the first Sherlock Holmes story, A Study in Scarlet
Chekhov's gun A detail shown early that must be used later Yes, it has to The power suit Ripley operates early in Aliens (1986)

Chekhov's advice to young playwrights, quoted on Wikipedia in several versions, comes down to this: if a rifle hangs on the wall in the first act, it should go off later. That rule is about wasting nothing. The MacGuffin goes the other way: the object can be almost anything, because the story is not really about it. Red herrings, as Wikipedia notes, are common in mystery fiction and often set up a plot twist.

How to spot a MacGuffin while watching

Try a simple swap test, based on StudioBinder's point that a MacGuffin is interchangeable: imagine the object replaced with something else. If stolen diamonds became stolen microfilm and the film would play almost the same, you are looking at a MacGuffin.

A few other signs help. The characters talk about it constantly but the camera rarely lingers on it. The story never fully explains what it is or what it does, as with the secrets in North by Northwest. And by the final scenes, what you remember is the people and the chase, not the prize.

Wikipedia calls the technique common in films, especially thrillers, which is why it is so often linked with Hitchcock. No Film School calls The Maltese Falcon a classic film noir; see what film noir is for that genre. Questions people ask

What is a MacGuffin in simple words?

It is the thing everyone in the story is after: a statue, a briefcase, a set of plans, a missing person. It gets the plot moving and gives the characters a reason to act, but the story is really about the chase, the people and the danger, so the object itself could be almost anything.

Who invented the word MacGuffin?

English screenwriter Angus MacPhail coined it, and Alfred Hitchcock adopted it and made it famous. Hitchcock explained it in a 1939 lecture at Columbia University and again in his 1966 interview with François Truffaut, both times with the same joke about a lion trap.

Is the briefcase in Pulp Fiction a MacGuffin?

Yes, it is one of the most quoted examples. The briefcase motivates several characters through the film's major plot points, and its contents are never revealed. You never need to know what is inside to follow the story.

What is the difference between a MacGuffin and a red herring?

A MacGuffin is something the characters really want, even if it is unimportant in itself. A red herring is a false clue that points the audience toward the wrong answer, as in mystery stories. The first drives the plot forward; the second sends you down a wrong path on purpose.

Can a person be a MacGuffin?

Yes. No Film School lists Private Ryan in Saving Private Ryan and the missing groom in The Hangover as people who work like MacGuffins: the whole plot is a search for them. George Lucas even called R2-D2 the MacGuffin of the first Star Wars film.
