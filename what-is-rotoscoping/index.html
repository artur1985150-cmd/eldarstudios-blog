In short

  • Rotoscoping is an animation technique in which artists trace over live-action footage, frame by frame, to get realistic movement.
  • Max Fleischer filed the patent for his rotoscope on 6 December 1915; it was granted on 9 October 1917.
  • Fleischer used it for the Out of the Inkwell cartoons, with his brother Dave in a clown costume as the live model.
  • In visual effects, rotoscoping also means drawing a matte around an object so it can be placed on another background.

Rotoscoping is an animation technique in which artists trace over live-action film, frame by frame, to make drawn characters move realistically. Max Fleischer invented the method and the device, called a rotoscope, in the mid-1910s: a projector beamed one film frame at a time onto glass, and the artist drew over it on paper. Computers have replaced the device, but the name stuck. In visual effects, the word also means cutting an object out of footage by hand.

What does rotoscoping mean?

Rotoscoping means drawing on top of filmed footage so the animation copies real movement. An actor or object is filmed first. Each frame is then traced, and the tracings become the frames of the cartoon.

The word comes from the rotoscope, Fleischer's machine. In his patent drawing, a film projector at one side beams a single frame onto a transparent easel, where the artist draws. Wikipedia describes the original setup as live-action images projected onto a glass panel and traced onto paper.

Today the term has two meanings, and it helps to keep them apart:

Meaning What the artist does Where you see it
Rotoscope animation Traces people or objects to create drawn animation Cartoons, animated features, music videos
Rotoscoping in visual effects Draws a matte (an outline) around an object in each frame to cut it out Live-action films, compositing, adding glow effects

Who invented the rotoscope, and when?

Max Fleischer, an animator born in Kraków in 1883 who worked in New York. The dates are often blurred, so here they are as the sources give them.

Fleischer Studios' own timeline says that in December 1915 Max filed a patent application for his invention of the rotoscope. Google Patents lists the patent as US 1,242,674, "Method of producing moving-picture cartoons", filed on 6 December 1915 and granted on 9 October 1917. When an article says "patented in 1915", it means the filing year. Wikipedia adds that the patent had expired by 1934, after which other producers could use the method freely.

The patent states its aim plainly: improved cartoon films showing figures "in a life-like manner". According to Fleischer Studios, the earliest experiments may have used footage of Boy Scouts signaling with semaphore flags. That footage no longer exists, but the patent drawings show it.

How did Fleischer use it in Out of the Inkwell?

Fleischer filmed his brother Dave dressed as a clown and traced the footage. The clown, later named Koko, became the star of Out of the Inkwell, which Wikipedia dates 1918 to 1927. On early screen credits the technique was called simply the "Fleischer Process", and for several years nobody else used it.

According to Fleischer Studios, Max joined Bray Studios in 1916, made Army training films during the First World War, and returned in 1918 to produce Out of the Inkwell films for the Bray Pictograph Screen Magazine. In 1921 Max and Dave left Bray and started their own company.

The shortcut turned out not to be short. Tracing had to be precise, and small gaps between the projected image and the drawing surface made lines wobble, so tracings had to be reworked. Wikipedia says Fleischer stopped depending on the rotoscope for fluid action by 1924, when Dick Huemer became animation director.

He came back to it in the 1930s for dance scenes in Betty Boop and Popeye cartoons, tracing jazz singer Cab Calloway's moves in Minnie the Moocher (1932), Snow-White (1933) and The Old Man of the Mountain (1933). There the tracing guided timing and position, while the cartoon characters were drawn in their own proportions. Fleischer also used it for the human characters of Gulliver's Travels (1939) and in the Superman cartoons of the early 1940s.

How does rotoscoping work step by step?

The old method and the digital one follow the same logic:

  1. Film the live action: an actor, a dancer, a horse, whatever needs to move realistically.
  2. Show one frame at a time on a drawing surface. Originally a projector sent the frame onto glass; now software shows it on screen.
  3. Trace the outlines you need, frame by frame, on a new sheet or layer each time.
  4. Clean up the tracings so the line stays steady and the character keeps its shape. Wikipedia notes that roto tracings are often redrawn using the tracing only as a guide.
  5. Color the drawings and shoot or render them in sequence.

For visual effects the steps are similar, but the result is a matte instead of a drawing. The original Star Wars trilogy used it for lightsabers: technicians traced a line over the prop sticks in each frame, then enlarged the line and added the glow.

Software has changed the slowest step. In the mid-1990s Bob Sabiston developed computer-assisted "interpolated rotoscoping", in which the program fills in some of the frames between the artist's drawings.

The Soviet Union used rotoscoping widely in early animated films, mostly for folk tales and poems such as The Night Before Christmas (1951). Video games use it too: Jordan Mechner rotoscoped the movement in Karateka and Prince of Persia.

Why does rotoscoped animation look the way it does?

Because the movement comes from a real body, rotoscoped characters move with a weight and rhythm that hand animation rarely copies exactly. That realism is why Disney traced Snow White and why Bakshi turned to it for battle scenes.

The same realism can feel odd. Drawn faces on real motion sit between cartoon and film, and lines that shift slightly from frame to frame give the image a shimmer. In Japan, The Flowers of Evil (2013) split viewers: some criticized the facial animation and the realism, while Anime News Network gave it a perfect score in its initial reactions. Two years later, The Case of Hana & Alice, animated entirely with the Rotoshop software, was much better received.

If you want to spot it, look for realistic body proportions and movement in a drawn world, and for outlines that wobble a little from frame to frame.

Is rotoscoping the same as motion capture?

No. Both start from a real performance, but they use it differently. Rotoscoping works on images: someone draws over each filmed frame, so the result is a drawing. Motion capture, as Wikipedia defines it, records the movement of actors into a computer and uses that data to animate digital character models, so nobody traces anything.

Animators still use both alongside the 12 principles of animation. A traced motion can be timed, exaggerated or simplified afterwards, which is what Fleischer did with Cab Calloway's dances.

Questions people ask

Who invented rotoscoping?

Max Fleischer, the animator behind Out of the Inkwell, Betty Boop and the Fleischer Popeye cartoons. He applied for a patent in December 1915 for a 'method of producing moving-picture cartoons' and it was granted in October 1917.

When was the rotoscope invented?

Fleischer developed it around 1915. Google Patents gives the filing date of his US patent 1,242,674 as 6 December 1915 and the grant date as 9 October 1917. Some articles say the patent dates from 1915; that is the filing year.

Is rotoscoping cheating?

It is a tool, and a slow one. Wikipedia notes that the process proved time-consuming because tracing must be precise, and the tracings usually need reworking so the line does not wobble. Animators often use it as a guide for timing and poses rather than copying every line.

What is the difference between rotoscoping and motion capture?

Rotoscoping works from filmed images: an artist draws over each frame. Motion capture records a performer's movement as data and applies it to a digital character. Both start from a real performance, but only rotoscoping involves drawing on top of the footage.

What movies use rotoscoping?

Examples include Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937), Ralph Bakshi's The Lord of the Rings (1978), the music video for a-ha's Take On Me (1985), and Richard Linklater's Waking Life (2001) and A Scanner Darkly (2006).
