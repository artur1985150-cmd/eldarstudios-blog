Film noir is a name for the dark, cynical crime dramas Hollywood made mainly in the 1940s and 1950s. They are known for shadowy, low-key black-and-white photography, morally compromised heroes, femmes fatales and stories of crime, corruption and bad luck. The term is French for "black film" and was first applied to Hollywood films by critic Nino Frank in 1946. Classic examples include The Maltese Falcon, Double Indemnity and Out of the Past.

What does film noir mean?

Film noir means "black film" in French, or more loosely "dark film". It describes a group of American crime films that share a bleak mood, a shadowy look and characters drawn into crime, often against their better judgment.

A definition quoted by Dartmouth's library from A Dictionary of Film Studies calls it a cycle of crime films made in Hollywood between 1940 and 1959. It lists the usual features: run-down districts of modern cities, a chiaroscuro world of night streets and dark interiors, psychologically disturbed men and deceitful women, convoluted plots with voice-over, flashbacks and ambiguous endings, and an overall world-weary cynicism.

The people who made these films did not use the word. Wikipedia notes that most American industry professionals of the time did not recognize the term, and that many films now called noir were described as melodramas when they came out. Historians and critics defined the category afterward, and it caught on widely in the 1970s.

Who coined the term film noir, and why is it French?

French critic Nino Frank applied it to Hollywood films in 1946. According to the Dartmouth guide, he used it in a review of four Hollywood crime thrillers: The Maltese Falcon (John Huston, 1941), Murder, My Sweet (Edward Dmytryk, 1944), Double Indemnity (Billy Wilder, 1944) and Laura (Otto Preminger, 1944). What struck him was their complex narration and moral ambivalence.

The French connection has a second layer. Frank is believed to have borrowed from Série noire, a French publishing imprint of crime novels founded in 1945. In 1955 the French critics Raymond Borde and Étienne Chaumeton wrote the first long study of the subject, Panorama du film noir américain 1941–1953. Their attempt at a definition called these films dreamlike, strange, erotic, ambivalent and cruel, while admitting that no single film has all five qualities in equal measure.

What are the main characteristics of film noir?

No single feature makes a film noir, and Wikipedia stresses that critics disagree on the list. Still, most classic noirs share several of these:

  1. Low-key lighting with strong contrasts of light and dark (chiaroscuro), and faces half lost in shadow.
  2. Shadows of Venetian blinds or banister rails falling across a person or a wall, a look that became a cliché early on.
  3. Low-angle, wide-angle and tilted (Dutch angle) shots, mirrors and distorting glass.
  4. Night scenes actually shot at night, and more location shooting from the mid-1940s.
  5. A protagonist under pressure: a private eye, a police officer, an aging boxer, a grifter, an ordinary citizen lured into crime, or simply a victim of circumstance.
  6. A femme fatale, though most noirs do not have one.
  7. Voice-over narration, flashbacks and twisting plots.
  8. A fatalistic mood. Endings are often bleak, though some classics, such as The Big Sleep, end happily.

The tilted camera has its own guide: what a Dutch angle is. The lighting comes from a painting term explained in what chiaroscuro is.

Where did film noir come from?

Its look and its stories have different roots. The look owes a lot to German Expressionism, the movement of the 1910s and 1920s; the stories come from American hardboiled crime fiction.

Many film artists who had worked in Germany emigrated to Hollywood, drawn by work and then pushed out by the threat of Nazism. Directors such as Fritz Lang, Robert Siodmak, Jacques Tourneur and Michael Curtiz brought a heavily shadowed style with them. Lang's M (1931), made in Germany, already joined a noirish look to a crime plot.

On the page, Dashiell Hammett's novels became The Maltese Falcon (1941) and The Glass Key (1942). James M. Cain's books became Double Indemnity (1944), Mildred Pierce (1945) and The Postman Always Rings Twice (1946). Raymond Chandler's novels became Murder, My Sweet and The Big Sleep, and Chandler worked on the script of Double Indemnity. Cornell Woolrich supplied more noir source material than anyone: thirteen films in the classic period.

Other influences are French poetic realism of the 1930s, with its doomed heroes, and, after the war, the location shooting of Italian neorealism. Technology helped too. From 1935 Eastman Kodak sold faster film stock that needed less light, which let cinematographers work with darker, shadow-filled scenes.

Many lesser-known noirs were cheap B movies and are hard to see today. The Film Noir Foundation, a non-profit, works to find and restore them. Since 2005 it has saved films including The Prowler (1951), Too Late for Tears (1949) and Woman on the Run (1950).

Is film noir a genre or a style?

Film scholars have argued about this for decades. Foster Hirsch says noir is a genre, with shared conventions of story, character, theme and look. Thomas Schatz calls it a style. Alain Silver, a leading American writer on noir, calls it a cycle and a phenomenon. Others call it a mood or simply a canon of films.

The argument against "genre" is that noir spans gangster films, police procedurals, gothic romances and social-problem pictures. It is not tied to a place, either: many classic noirs are set in small towns, suburbs or on the open road rather than in the big city. Wikipedia's summary is blunt: there is no consensus.

For a viewer the debate matters less than the feel. If a crime film is shot in shadow, expects the worst of its characters and does not promise a happy ending, people will call it noir.

What is neo-noir, and are noir films still made?

Neo-noir is the name for films made after the classic period that revisit noir's themes and look, often knowingly.

Wikipedia calls Roman Polanski's Chinatown (1974) the most acclaimed neo-noir of its era. Martin Scorsese's Taxi Driver (1976) moved the noir attitude into the present day, and Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982), which pays homage to classic noir, is the film Wikipedia names as most directly influential on cyberpunk.

Directors keep coming back to it. The Coen brothers made Blood Simple (1984) and the black-and-white The Man Who Wasn't There (2001). Michael Mann's Heat (1995) belongs to what some call "neon noir", with contemporary settings, stylized lighting and rock or hip-hop soundtracks.

Noir also crossed into animation. Wikipedia describes the Fleischer studio's Superman cartoons of the early 1940s as action-oriented film noir, made with the help of rotoscoping. Questions people ask

What does film noir mean?

It is French for 'black film', closer in meaning to 'dark film'. French critic Nino Frank used it in 1946 for a group of Hollywood crime thrillers. Hollywood itself did not use the term at the time; most of these films were sold as melodramas.

What was the first film noir?

Stranger on the Third Floor (1940), directed by Boris Ingster, is the film most commonly cited as the first true film noir. The Maltese Falcon (1941) is widely regarded as the first major noir of the classic era.

Is film noir a genre or a style?

Critics still disagree. Foster Hirsch argues it is a genre with shared tone, visual style and narrative; Thomas Schatz treats it as a style, and Alain Silver calls it a cycle. There is no consensus.

Are all film noirs black and white?

Most classic noirs are, but not all. Leave Her to Heaven (1945) and Niagara (1953) are color films routinely included in noir filmographies.

What is neo-noir?

Neo-noir is the name for later films that bring back noir's themes and style, usually from the 1960s onward. Chinatown (1974), Taxi Driver (1976) and Blade Runner (1982) are well-known examples.
