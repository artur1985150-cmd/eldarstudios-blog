In short

  • Foley is everyday sound (footsteps, clothing, doors, glass) recorded after filming, in sync with the picture.
  • It is named after Jack Foley, who made sound for films from the late 1920s until his death in 1967.
  • Foley sounds fall into three groups: feet, moves and specifics.
  • Stand-in props are common: coconut shells for hooves, cornstarch in a leather pouch for snow.
  • Good Foley goes unnoticed; without it a film sounds strangely empty.

Foley is the everyday sound in a film that is recorded after shooting, by an artist who watches the picture and performs each noise in time with it: footsteps, rustling clothes, a door, a glass set on a table. It is named after Jack Foley, a sound effects artist who worked at Universal. Good Foley is meant to go unnoticed. You only miss it when it is gone and a scene sounds unnaturally quiet.

What does Foley mean in film?

Foley means reproducing ordinary sounds and adding them to a film, video or game in post-production. Wikipedia lists the range: anything from swishing clothing and footsteps to squeaky doors and breaking glass. The place where it happens is called a Foley stage or Foley studio, and the person doing it is a Foley artist.

The point is realism. ACMI describes Foley as a technique for recording audio "that syncs to the on-screen action". Viewers should believe the sound was captured together with the picture, even though it was not.

Foley is not the only sound in a film. The Los Angeles Film School calls it one layer, working alongside dialogue, music, ambient tracks and other sound effects.

Why do films need Foley at all?

Because the sound recorded on set is often unusable or unconvincing. Wikipedia says the original sounds are very often obstructed by noise, or are not convincing enough for the action on screen.

It gives these typical reasons:

  • Fight scenes are staged by stunt performers, so no blows actually land and there is nothing to record.
  • Props, sets and costumes are often built from cheap, light materials that look like the real thing but do not sound like it.
  • Unwanted noise, such as planes overhead or passing traffic, spoils the on-set track and has to be covered.
  • Recording these sounds separately gives the editors full control over how each one sounds, its quality and its volume.

There is one more reason, and it matters to anyone who watches films in translation. When a film is dubbed, the actors' voices are muted for the voice actors. Every incidental sound captured by the same equipment on location is lost along with them. Wikipedia says the need for Foley rose dramatically once studios began to distribute films in countries that required dubbing.

Who was Jack Foley?

Jack Donovan Foley (1891 to 1967) was an American sound effects artist. He joined Universal in the silent era, in jobs from stuntman to director. Foley became part of the crew that turned the silent musical Show Boat (1929) into a sound film, after Universal called for staff with radio experience.

Microphones of the time could not pick up more than dialogue, so other sounds had to be added after shooting. Foley and his small crew projected the film on a screen and performed the effects live onto a single audio track. Their timing had to be exact, so that footsteps and closing doors matched the actors' movements. Wikipedia credits him with voicing the first Tarzan yell, in Tarzan the Tiger (1929), and with work on Dracula (1931) and Spartacus (1960).

He received the Golden Reel Award from the Motion Picture Sound Editors. By his own calculation, he walked over 5,000 miles making footstep sounds for films.

The technique is older than his name for it. Radio studios in the early 1920s were already making sound effects live for radio dramas, because recordings of the time could not play effects on cue.

How is Foley made? Feet, moves and specifics

Foley artists watch the film, work out which sounds a scene needs, gather props and practise. Then they perform while the picture plays, much as actors re-record dialogue in sync. According to the Los Angeles Film School, they usually come in once the picture edit is locked.

Wikipedia splits the work into three groups:

Group What it covers How it is done
Feet Footsteps Studios keep many kinds of shoes and floors ("Foley pits"), from marble squares to gravel and rock
Moves Subtle sounds such as clothing swishing as two actors pass Rubbing two pieces of the same material near the microphone at the rate the legs cross
Specifics Other sounds, such as doors closing and doorbells ringing Often done more efficiently with stock sound effects arranged by sound editors

Artists who work on footsteps are sometimes called Foley walkers or steppers. Today each sound can be captured on its own track and lined up precisely, rather than everything being performed onto one track as in Jack Foley's day.

Famous Foley tricks and props

Studios use many stand-ins. Wikipedia lists cornstarch in a leather pouch for crunching snow, gloves for bird wings, padded coconut shell halves for horses' hooves and frozen romaine lettuce for cracking bones. Cellophane makes a crackling fire. Walnuts stand in for ice cubes in a glass, because they do not melt and can be reused.

Film-specific stories are popular too:

  • Titanic (1997): the crackle of Rose's frozen hair was frozen lettuce being peeled, according to the Los Angeles Film School.
  • Raiders of the Lost Ark: Musicbed writes that the punches were a baseball bat hitting a pile of leather jackets.
  • Fight Club (1999): the Los Angeles Film School lists chicken carcasses shattered with baseball bats, cracking walnuts and heavy bags thrown to the floor.

Accounts of the same sound differ in detail. For E.T., ACMI mentions jelly, popcorn and liver, and also Jell-O poured into a taped-up T-shirt. The Los Angeles Film School names only Jell-O and a damp T-shirt.

Star Wars belongs on this list with a caveat. Wikipedia says Ben Burtt's lightsaber buzz was a film projector motor mixed with television picture tube hum. Musicbed notes that much of Star Wars is sound design, but that Foley was essential too, for Jedi robes, holster grabs and footsteps.

Monty Python and the Holy Grail made a joke out of the trade: its knights pretend to ride while their squires follow, clapping coconut halves.

Foley vs sound effects vs sound design

These terms overlap. Here is how the sources use them:

  • Foley is performed by a person, in sync with the picture, usually everyday sounds. Musicbed calls it "tactile".
  • Digital sound effects, in Musicbed's description, are pre-recorded clips from a sound library or made with software. Wikipedia adds that doorbells are usually done with stock effects.
  • Sound design is the wider job. Musicbed says much of Star Wars is sound design, with Foley added on top.

Modern films blend these. Musicbed says most modern sound design blends Foley and digital effects, and Wikipedia says the soundscape of most films combines Foley with sound recorded on set.

How to hear Foley while you watch

Foley is designed to disappear, so listening for it takes some effort. A few places to try:

  1. Films built on silence. Musicbed calls A Quiet Place (2018) a masterclass in Foley, because in a film where everyone must stay silent every small sound is magnified.
  2. Footsteps. Foley studios keep many shoes and floors, from marble to gravel, so footsteps are a good place to listen for the craft.
  3. Animation. For Inside Out (2015), ACMI says the team recorded crabs on sand and kelp underwater. If you are curious how animated films are planned shot by shot, see types of animation.
  4. Dubbed films. When a film is dubbed, the incidental sounds recorded on location are lost with the original voices, as Wikipedia explains. So the small sounds under a dubbed voice had to be supplied again.

Foley also helps you judge the size of a space. Wikipedia notes that a large hall has strong reverberation and a small room only slight reverberation.

Foley beyond film

Foley began in radio. Wikipedia says what is now called Foley originated in live radio drama in the early 1920s, and that Foley sounds are used today in film, audio dramas and video games.

Questions people ask

What is Foley in simple words?

It is the small, ordinary sound in a film that was added later by a person watching the screen: footsteps, a coat brushing past, a cup set down, a punch landing. The artist performs each sound in time with the actors so that it seems to have been recorded on set.

Why is it called Foley?

After Jack Foley, a Universal employee who helped turn the silent musical Show Boat into a sound film in 1929 and kept making sound for films until his death in 1967. His method of performing effects live to the projected picture is still the basis of the job, and the people who do it are called Foley artists.

What is the difference between Foley and sound effects?

Foley is performed by a person in sync with the picture, usually everyday sounds made with props. Other sound effects can be pre-recorded clips from libraries or made with software, as Musicbed describes it. Modern sound design usually blends the two.

Is Foley the same as a Foley catheter?

No. The Foley catheter is a urinary catheter used in medicine, named after Frederic Foley, who designed the original in 1929. Film Foley is named after a different man, the sound artist Jack Foley. The two only share a surname.

Is sound built for animated films too?

Yes. For the animated film Inside Out (2015), a sound editor put microphones on sand and recorded small crabs scuttling along. According to ACMI and the Los Angeles Film School, the result became the ambient sound of Riley's mind.
