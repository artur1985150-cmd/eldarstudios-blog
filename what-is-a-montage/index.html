A montage is a film editing technique in which a series of short shots is cut together to condense space, time and information. Instead of showing events in full, the film jumps between moments, often over music, so that weeks of training or years of a life pass in a minute or two. The word is French for assembly or editing, and it has three slightly different meanings depending on whose cinema you are talking about.

What does montage mean in film?

The plain definition is the one above: short shots, put in sequence, to say a lot in little screen time. StudioBinder adds that the shots can be moving or still, and that montages usually lean on music, quick cuts, voiceover and little or no dialogue.

The trouble is that the word travels between film cultures and changes on the way. Wikipedia lists three uses:

Where What "montage" means Typical example
France Simply editing, any editing Every French film credit for the editor
Soviet cinema, 1920s Making new meaning by colliding shots Workers and a slaughtered bull in Strike
Hollywood and British studios A "montage sequence" that shows time passing The training run in Rocky (1976)

When a viewer today says "there's a montage in this film", they almost always mean the third kind. When a film critic writes about "montage" in Eisenstein, they mean the second. Both are right.

Where does the word come from?

It is French. No Film School traces it to the verb monter, to mount or assemble: the editor literally mounts strips of film one after another. In French film work montage still means editing in general, the same way English uses "cutting".

The narrower sense came from Russia. According to the Wikipedia article on Soviet montage theory, Lev Kuleshov first used the term montage to explain how a succession of shots works, in an essay published in the journal Vestnik Kinematografii in 1916.

Soviet montage and the Kuleshov effect

For the Soviet directors of the 1920s, montage was the whole point of cinema. Sergei Eisenstein called it "the nerve of cinema" and held that meaning comes from the collision of independent shots.

In his film Strike, shots of striking workers being attacked are cut with a bull being slaughtered. Neither shot says "the workers are treated like cattle". The cut says it.

Eisenstein sorted montage into kinds: metric (cutting by a set number of frames), rhythmic (cutting to the movement inside the shots, as in the Odessa Steps sequence of Battleship Potemkin), tonal, overtonal and intellectual. You do not need the list to watch his films, but it shows how deliberately the cuts were planned.

The idea behind all of this is the Kuleshov effect. Kuleshov is said to have cut the same expressionless close-up of the actor Ivan Mosjoukine next to three other shots: a bowl of soup, a girl in a coffin and a woman on a divan. Audiences praised the actor for showing hunger, grief and desire. The face was the same each time.

Here is the less tidy part. Kuleshov and his collaborator Vsevolod Pudovkin told differing versions of the experiment, and no film or photo of it is known to survive. When Stephen Prince and Wayne Hensley recreated it in 1992, they did not find the effect. Later studies did: an fMRI study by Dean Mobbs and colleagues in 2006, and a 2016 study by Daniel Barratt and colleagues with 36 participants, both found that a neutral face is read through the shot around it.

Eisenstein himself credited Abel Gance with sparking his interest in montage. Gance's Napoléon (1927) uses montage throughout and ends on a triptych: three reels of film side by side, showing one wide image or three separate montages.

The Hollywood montage sequence

American and British studios took the word and used it for something smaller and more practical: a sequence that suggests time passing. From the 1930s to the 1950s these sequences mixed short shots with optical effects such as dissolves, split screens and double or triple exposures, plus dance and music.

Studios even had montage specialists. Slavko Vorkapić at MGM was the best-known montage maker of the 1930s; one of his jobs was showing Jeanette MacDonald's rise to opera fame in Maytime (1937). Don Siegel, later a well-known director, ran the montage department at Warner Bros. from 1933 to 1942 and made montage sequences for hundreds of features, Casablanca among them.

Two stock devices came out of this era. The newsreel montage shows newspapers spinning off the presses and headlines flying at the camera, as in Citizen Kane. The railroad montage shows engines rushing toward the lens, wheels turning and destination signs filling the screen. Once you know them, they are easy to spot in old films.

Famous montage examples

The training montage is the type most people picture first. Wikipedia describes its usual shape: the hero faces their lack of preparation, starts a hard training routine, and is shown improving in short cuts while an energetic song, often rock, plays as the only sound.

By the end several weeks have passed in a few minutes. The classic is Rocky (1976), which ends with Rocky's run up the steps of the Philadelphia Museum of Art. The form spread far beyond sport, to flying in Armageddon (1998), fighting in Batman Begins (2005), magic in Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix (2007) and public speaking in The King's Speech (2010). StudioBinder adds the training of the recruits in Mulan, who start hopeless and end as soldiers.

It became so familiar that it turned into a cliché, and comedy took it apart. South Park's episode "Asspen" plays a training montage whose song spells out the rules of training montages while they happen on screen, and Team America: World Police does a similar joke.

Other montages do different jobs:

  • Cross-cutting for meaning. In The Godfather, StudioBinder points out, the baptism of Michael Corleone's nephew is cut together with the killings of rival mafia leaders, so the ceremony and the murders comment on each other.
  • Several storylines at once. The Big Short cuts between its characters as they all react to the same market event.
  • Character in a hurry. The opening titles of 500 Days of Summer sketch the two leads' childhoods in short shots.
  • Collision, Soviet style. Wikipedia's Soviet montage article notes that the end of Apocalypse Now places the killing of Colonel Kurtz next to a village ritual in which a water buffalo is slaughtered.
  • A finale. Bonnie and Clyde (1967) is known for its montage ending, edited by Dede Allen.

The montage in Up: a whole marriage without words

One of the most praised montages of this century is in an animated film. Pixar's Up opened the Cannes Film Festival on 13 May 2009, and its opening sequence, often called "Married Life" after the music, follows Carl and Ellie's life together up to Ellie's death.

The filmmakers needed a reason for an old man to float his house away on balloons, so they decided to show the whole marriage first. They pictured it as a wordless montage that plays like a set of home movies. The sequence is only minutes long and almost silent: watching clouds, saving coins in a jar that keeps getting broken open for repairs, Ellie tying Carl's tie through the years, until the last tie is a bow tie.

The music does the talking. "Married Life" was composer Michael Giacchino's first assignment on the film, and director Pete Docter asked for something that would sound like a grandmother's music box. The score went on to win the Academy Award for Best Original Score. How to spot a montage while you watch

Most editing in a film tries to be invisible. A montage is editing you are meant to notice. Signs you are inside one:

  • The shots are short and jump in time or place, but the film does not treat the jumps as scene changes.
  • Music carries the sequence, and there is little or no dialogue.
  • Something visibly changes from the first shot to the last: a body gets stronger, a house gets older, a city gets built.
  • When it ends, the story has skipped ahead, and nobody on screen remarks on it.

If the shots are not skipping time but placed side by side to make you think something neither shot says alone, you are looking at the older, Soviet kind. That is worth knowing when a film puts two images together and the meaning arrives a second later. It is the same trick a plot twist uses on a larger scale: what you already saw changes once the next piece arrives.

Montage, collage and video essays

Montage is related to two other forms. Wikipedia points to the collage film, made by assembling found footage, and the video essay, which argues a point by cutting clips together. Both live on the same idea Kuleshov described: the order of the shots makes the meaning.

Montage is also not limited to live action. Animation can compress years into a song as easily as a camera can. For the different ways animated films are made, see types of animation. Questions people ask

What is a montage in simple words?

It is a quick run of short shots, usually over music, that shows a lot happening in a little screen time. Weeks of training, years of a marriage or a city waking up can pass in a minute or two because the editor jumps between moments instead of showing them in full.

Where does the word montage come from?

From French, where it means assembly or editing; No Film School traces it to the verb monter, to mount or assemble. In French film talk montage still just means editing. In English it came to mean the special time-squeezing sequence.

What is the Kuleshov effect?

It is the idea that viewers read meaning from how two shots are joined. Lev Kuleshov reportedly cut one blank face of actor Ivan Mosjoukine next to soup, a girl in a coffin and a woman on a divan, and audiences saw hunger, grief and desire. No film of it survives; studies in 2006 and 2016 found the effect.

What is a training montage?

A short sequence in which a character trains or learns something over quick cuts, usually with a fast song and no dialogue, so that weeks pass in minutes. It started in American cinema; the best-known example is in Rocky (1976), ending with Rocky's run up the steps of the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

What is the difference between a montage and editing?

Every film is edited, but most editing hides itself and keeps scenes flowing in real time. A montage is a stretch of editing you notice: the shots are short, often unconnected in time or place, and the meaning comes from their order. In French the two words are the same.
