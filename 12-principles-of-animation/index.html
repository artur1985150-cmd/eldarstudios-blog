In short

  • The 12 principles of animation are a list of techniques for making drawn or computer-made characters move in a believable way.
  • Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston set them out in their 1981 book The Illusion of Life.
  • The principles grew out of the work of Disney animators from the 1930s on, in films such as Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, Pinocchio and Bambi.
  • They were written for hand-drawn animation, but 3D animators, motion designers and game animators still learn them.

The 12 principles of animation are a set of techniques that make animated characters seem to have weight, follow the laws of physics and show feeling. Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston set them out in their 1981 book The Illusion of Life. The twelve are squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight ahead action and pose to pose, follow through and overlapping action, slow in and slow out, arc, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing and appeal.

Where do the 12 principles of animation come from?

They come from the Disney studio. Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston were two of Walt Disney's "Nine Old Men", his key animators for decades.

In 1981 they published Disney Animation: The Illusion of Life with Abbeville Press, a 576-page history of how Disney animation was made. The third chapter lays out the twelve principles.

The techniques are older than the book. According to Wikipedia, the list is based on the work of Disney animators from the 1930s onward, who were trying to make animation look more realistic. The Arlington Museum of Art points to Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937), Pinocchio (1940) and Bambi (1942) as films in which they were worked out.

The book later came out under a reversed title, The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation, in a 1995 edition from Disney's Hyperion. In 1999 it topped an internet poll of the best animation books of all time, run by Animation-Books.com with the magazine ANiMATO! and reported by Animation World Network. Some people call it the "bible of animation".

What are the 12 principles of animation?

Here is the full list in the usual order, with what each one does and what you would notice as a viewer. Names vary a little between sources: the Arlington Museum of Art, for instance, says "ease in and ease out" and "timing and spacing".

# Principle What it does What you see on screen
1 Squash and stretch Gives weight and flexibility A ball flattens when it lands and stretches as it falls
2 Anticipation Prepares the audience for an action A character crouches before jumping
3 Staging Makes the main idea of a shot clear Light, framing and pose point you at one thing
4 Straight ahead action and pose to pose Two ways of drawing a scene Wild, loose motion or carefully planned poses
5 Follow through and overlapping action Loose parts keep moving after the body stops Hair, ears and coat tails swing and settle
6 Slow in and slow out Movement speeds up and slows down A car eases away rather than jumping to full speed
7 Arc Natural motion follows curves An arm swings in a curve, not a straight line
8 Secondary action Smaller actions support the main one A walking character also whistles or swings their arms
9 Timing Number of frames per action sets speed and weight Heavy things react slowly, light things fast
10 Exaggeration Pushes reality further so it reads Motion pushed further than real life, without losing believability
11 Solid drawing Shapes feel three-dimensional Characters have volume, weight and balance
12 Appeal The character is interesting to watch Even the villain holds your attention

What do the first six principles mean?

Squash and stretch gives an object weight. The classic exercise is a bouncing ball that flattens on impact and stretches as it falls. Wikipedia stresses one rule: in realistic animation the volume stays the same, so if the ball gets taller it must also get thinner.

Anticipation is the small move before the big one. A dancer bends the knees before leaving the floor; a golfer swings the club back before hitting. It can also be quieter, such as a character glancing off-screen just before someone walks in.

Staging borrows from theatre and film. Thomas and Johnston defined it as presenting an idea so that it is "completely and unmistakably clear". Placement in the frame, light and shadow, and camera angle all help.

Straight ahead action and pose to pose are two working methods. Straight ahead means drawing frame after frame from start to finish, which gives fluid, unpredictable motion. Pose to pose means drawing the key poses first and filling the gaps later. Animators often mix the two.

Follow through and overlapping action deal with the parts that lag behind. When a character stops, hair, clothes and loose skin keep moving and then settle. Overlapping action means different body parts move on different timing. A "moving hold", such as a character breathing while sitting still, keeps a pause from looking dead.

Slow in and slow out is about acceleration. Animators put more drawings near the start and end of a movement and fewer in the middle, so the motion speeds up and slows down the way real objects do.

What do the last six principles mean?

Arc says that most natural movement travels along curves. A turning head or a thrown ball follows an arc; only machines tend to move in straight lines. When a fingertip jumps off its arc between two drawings, the motion looks jittery.

Secondary action adds life without stealing focus. A person walking may also swing their arms or whistle. If the extra action distracts from the main one, it should go.

Timing is the number of drawings or frames given to an action, which becomes its speed on screen. It shows weight (a light object reacts to a push faster than a heavy one) and it also shows mood and personality.

Exaggeration keeps animation from feeling flat. Disney's version stays true to reality but in a wilder, more extreme form. Wikipedia adds a warning about balance: if everything in a scene is pushed equally hard, the viewer gets confused.

Solid drawing means thinking in three dimensions: anatomy, weight, balance, light and shadow. Thomas and Johnston warned against "twins", characters whose left and right sides mirror each other exactly and look lifeless.

Appeal is the animated equivalent of an actor's charisma. A villain can have appeal too. What matters is that the viewer finds the character real and interesting.

Which principle of animation is the most important?

The book does not rank them, and the principles work together. A bouncing ball uses squash and stretch, slow in and slow out, arcs and timing at once.

Timing gets singled out most. The Los Angeles Film School calls it the one principle that survives every technology shift untouched, because whoever sets the keys is still deciding how fast a movement should feel. Anticipation, staging and appeal also hold up well, since they describe how an audience's eye reads motion rather than how pencils and paper work.

Do the 12 principles still apply to 3D animation?

They do, with some changes. The list was written for hand-drawn work. Wikipedia cites a 1987 paper by John Lasseter, "Principles of traditional animation applied to 3D computer animation", and notes that the principles remain highly relevant to computer animation.

Some principles shift in meaning. LA Film School points out that a 3D rig can keep a character's volume automatically, so squash and stretch becomes a deliberate choice instead of a fix for flat drawings. Solid drawing moves toward posing and silhouette, because the 3D model already has volume. Computers can fill in the frames between key poses (the job of tweening), but Wikipedia adds that someone still has to oversee the result and apply the other principles.

The same school's article warns about two misreadings. Follow through gets reduced to "add a bounce curve" in software, and automatic hair and cloth simulation can turn secondary action into noise that hides the main action.

How can you spot the principles when you watch a cartoon?

You do not need to draw to see them. Try these the next time you watch any animated film or series:

  1. Watch a character land after a jump. Does the body squash a little and the hair keep moving after the feet stop?
  2. Look for the wind-up before a punch, a throw or a run. That is anticipation.
  3. Pause on a busy scene. If you know at once where to look, the staging is working.
  4. Follow a hand or a head across the frame. Smooth curves mean arcs; robotic straight lines usually mean the animators chose a mechanical look on purpose.
  5. Compare a heavy character with a light one. Timing should make the heavy one slower to start and stop.

The principles show up in every technique, from hand-drawn films to stop motion and claymation. Questions people ask

Who created the 12 principles of animation?

Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, two of the Disney animators later called the Nine Old Men, introduced the list in their 1981 book Disney Animation: The Illusion of Life. The techniques themselves had been developed at the Disney studio from the 1930s onward.

What are the 12 principles of animation in order?

The usual order is squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight ahead action and pose to pose, follow through and overlapping action, slow in and slow out, arc, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing and appeal.

What is the most important principle of animation?

The book does not rank them. The Los Angeles Film School singles out timing as the one principle that survives every change of technology.

Do the 12 principles apply to 3D animation?

Yes. Wikipedia notes that the principles still have great relevance for computer animation. Computers can fill in the frames between key poses, but someone still has to decide timing, arcs and staging.
