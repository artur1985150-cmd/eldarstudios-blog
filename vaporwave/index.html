Vaporwave is an internet music genre and visual aesthetic that appeared in the early 2010s. Its music is made from slowed-down, looped samples of 1980s and 1990s smooth jazz, lounge, elevator music and R&B. Its images borrow from the same years: Greek statues, early Windows graphics, glitchy VHS tape, anime and pink-and-teal neon. Somewhere between a joke and a critique of consumer culture, it sounds like a mall you half remember.

What is vaporwave?

Vaporwave is both a sound and a picture, and the two are hard to separate. Wikipedia calls it an internet music microgenre, an aesthetic and a meme at once. Academic Laura Glitsos notes that it breaks the usual rule of music coming first: here the visuals matter as much as the tracks.

The name is a pun on "vaporware", software that is announced but never shipped. That suits the raw material. Vaporwave artists take music that was made to set a mood rather than to be listened to, such as corporate stock music, infomercial jingles and hotel-lobby jazz, and stretch it out until it turns strange.

Bandcamp Daily describes vaporwave as an approach and an attitude as much as a genre: it repackages forgotten pop culture and adds a layer of social commentary. Fans and critics still argue whether that commentary is sincere, ironic or both.

Where did vaporwave come from?

Vaporwave grew out of the online music scenes of 2009 to 2011, as an ironic cousin of chillwave and hypnagogic pop. Three records built it. Daniel Lopatin released Chuck Person's Eccojams Vol. 1 in August 2010, James Ferraro released Far Side Virtual in October 2011, and Ramona Langley released Floral Shoppe as Macintosh Plus in December 2011.

Each added something. Eccojams looped and slowed 1980s pop hits under heavy echo; its packaging imitated the 1993 video game Ecco the Dolphin. Far Side Virtual drew on the bright beeps of then-recent technology like Skype, Second Life, Windows XP and the Nintendo Wii. Floral Shoppe combined all the core elements and became the record most people mean when they say vaporwave.

The word itself first turned up in an October 2011 blog post reviewing an album by Girlhood. Early artists hid behind fake corporate names and gave their music away on Bandcamp, SoundCloud and similar sites.

How did vaporwave spread online?

Vaporwave reached a wider audience in the middle of 2012, through Last.fm, Reddit, 4chan and Tumblr, where people decorated their pages with its imagery. In November 2012 the YouTuber Anthony Fantano reviewed Floral Shoppe, which fixed it as the genre's defining album. Soon after, fans began calling the genre "dead".

It did not die. From 2013, when YouTube allowed live streams, 24-hour "radio stations" for vaporwave and lo-fi hip hop appeared. Swedish rapper Yung Lean and his Sad Boys collective inspired mixes built on Nintendo 64 music, with titles like "Mariowave". In 2015 MTV International introduced a rebrand heavily inspired by vaporwave and seapunk.

By 2016 vaporwave albums, Floral Shoppe among them, still ranked among the best-selling experimental albums on Bandcamp. In September 2019 musician George Clanton organized the first vaporwave festival, 100% ElectroniCON, in New York City.

What does vaporwave art look like?

Vaporwave art looks like a screensaver from 1995 left running too long. Wikipedia lists its building blocks: 1990s web design, glitch art, cyberpunk tropes, anime, Greco-Roman statues, the geometric shapes of 1980s Memphis design, 3D-rendered objects and the wobble of a worn VHS tape.

A few recurring details help you read it:

  • text set in wide "fullwidth" characters, such as the famous ＡＥＳＴＨＥＴＩＣＳ;
  • marble busts floating over checkerboard floors or palm trees;
  • Windows 95-style windows, error boxes and early 3D grids;
  • Japanese text and 1980s and 1990s anime stills;
  • pink, purple and teal, often with a sunset gradient.

There is also a time limit. Artists usually take their sources from the years between Japan's 1980s economic boom and 2001, the year of the September 11 attacks and the dot-com crash. The cover of Floral Shoppe shows the Twin Towers still standing. The feel overlaps with liminal spaces, those empty malls and corridors that look familiar and wrong at once.

Where does vaporwave show up in animation and anime?

The clearest crossover is Simpsonwave. In late 2015 a user called Spicster posted a Vine that set clips of The Simpsons to the track "Resonance" by HOME. It went viral, and in 2016 editors such as Lucien Hughes pushed the style further with VHS distortion and surreal, out-of-context scenes.

Anime runs through the whole genre. The offshoot called future funk is built on 1980s and 1990s anime imagery, with reference points such as Urusei Yatsura, Super Dimension Fortress Macross, Kimagure Orange Road and Sailor Moon. Its music samples Japanese city pop records, and Wikipedia credits it with bringing city pop to Western listeners. Macross 82-99 pioneered the style with his Sailorwave album series in 2013. For the medium behind those stills, see our guide to what anime is.

Video games feed it as well: the packaging of Eccojams resembled the 1993 game Ecco the Dolphin, and the mid-2010s mixes inspired by Yung Lean borrowed both the music and the imagery of Nintendo 64 games.

What are the main vaporwave subgenres?

Fans have split vaporwave into many branches, some in earnest and some as a joke. Complex writer Joe Price noted that most faded away and that the look often formed faster than the sound. The ones below are the branches Bandcamp Daily and Wikipedia treat as lasting.

What is vaporwave saying?

Vaporwave's message is deliberately unclear. In a 2012 Dummy article, critic Adam Harper wrote that its producers could be read as sarcastic anti-capitalists or as willing fans of consumer culture "shivering with delight". Numerous academic books have taken up the question since.

Bandcamp Daily hears it as a pop-culture movement that criticizes pop culture: songs like Cyndi Lauper's "Time After Time" are celebrated, mocked and condemned at the same time. Mallsoft, for example, plays with the idea that "the mall" has become a state of mind.

That ambiguity also left room for misuse. Around 2015 a far-right offshoot called fashwave borrowed vaporwave and synthwave visuals, which Wikipedia describes as a separate, extremist fusion rather than part of the original scene.

How is vaporwave related to other aesthetics?

Vaporwave is one of several internet aesthetics built on nostalgia. Its closest neighbor is the Y2K aesthetic, which picks up roughly where vaporwave's sources stop, around the year 2000, with chrome and translucent plastic. Its opposite in mood is cottagecore, which longs for gardens and bread instead of malls and computers.

The dark, neon cities of cyberpunk also feed vaporwave's images. You can find all of these in our aesthetics section.

Questions people ask

What is vaporwave in simple terms?

It is music made by slowing down and looping old pop, smooth jazz and lounge recordings, paired with images of the same era: Greek busts, early computer graphics, shopping malls and VHS noise. The result sounds like a half-remembered advert from the 1990s.

Why is it called vaporwave?

The name plays on 'vaporware', a term for commercial software that is announced but never released. It fits a genre made from the leftovers of corporate music, ads and technology. The first reported use of the word was in an October 2011 blog post.

What is the most famous vaporwave album?

Floral Shoppe, released in December 2011 by Ramona Langley under the name Macintosh Plus. It is considered the first album to contain all of the genre's core elements, and a November 2012 video review by Anthony Fantano made it the genre's representative record.

Is vaporwave dead?

People have called it dead since about 2012, often fans themselves. Yet vaporwave albums still ranked among Bandcamp's best-selling experimental records in 2016, and the first vaporwave festival, 100% ElectroniCON, took place in New York in September 2019.

What is Simpsonwave?

A mid-2010s offshoot that pairs clips from The Simpsons with vaporwave tracks. It started in late 2015 with an edit by a user called Spicster set to 'Resonance' by HOME, and in 2016 editors such as Lucien Hughes added VHS distortion and dreamlike visuals.
