Gouache is a water-based paint made of pigment, water and a binder, usually gum arabic or dextrin, and designed to be opaque. Think of it as watercolor made thick: it has more pigment and often a white filler such as chalk, so the white of the paper does not show through. That is why it is also called body color or opaque watercolor. It dries fast to a flat, matte finish and can be rewetted with water.

What is gouache made of?

The ingredients are almost the same as watercolor. Tate describes it as pigments bound in a water-soluble gum, like watercolor, with a white pigment added to make it opaque. Gouache uses a larger share of binder than watercolor, plus inert fillers such as chalk to make it cover better.

Wikipedia adds the details. The traditional binder is gum arabic; since the late 19th century, cheaper paints have used yellow dextrin instead. Makers often add propylene glycol so the dry layer is less brittle and sticks better. Compared with watercolor, the pigment particles are larger and the ratio of pigment to binder is much higher. The result is a heavier paint that reflects more light.

Gouache is also sold in a newer form, acrylic gouache. It has the same dense pigment but an acrylic binder, with additives that keep it matte. Unlike traditional gouache, it becomes water-resistant once dry.

Gouache vs watercolor vs acrylic

All three can sit in the same art shop aisle and all three thin with water, so they get mixed up. The differences show on the paper.

Two of these traits matter if you only look at paintings. Gouache can put a pale color on top of a dark one, which watercolor cannot do cleanly. And gouache dries to a value different from the wet paint: light tones dry darker and dark tones dry lighter. Wikipedia notes this makes it hard to match colors across several painting sessions, which is one reason gouache pictures are often finished quickly.

Many watercolor makers also produce gouache, and the two are easy to use together.

Where does the word gouache come from?

English took the word from French, and French took it from the Italian guazzo, which means "mud". In Italy guazzo first named an early 16th-century practice of painting oil over a tempera base, which could give a matte look.

In 18th-century France the word gouache came to mean opaque water-based paint, and Tate dates the first use of the term to France in that century.

The word also means a painting made this way. "A gouache" on a museum label means a picture painted this way.

As for pronunciation, the English versions are "goo-AHSH" and "GWAHSH", the second one close to the French.

A short history of gouache

The paint is much older than its name. Wikipedia says it has been in use for at least twelve centuries:

  • In ancient Egyptian painting a form of gouache was bound with honey or tragacanth gum.
  • Makers of European illuminated manuscripts and Persian miniatures used it. Persian and Mughal miniatures are often called watercolors, but most of them are gouache.
  • Tate says opaque water-based paint, called bodycolour, was used by artists from the late 15th century. Lead white was the usual white until zinc oxide, known as Chinese white, arrived in the 19th century.
  • In the 18th century artists used gouache to add fine detail to pastel paintings, and it was an important medium for the Italian landscape painters Marco Ricci and Francesco Zuccarelli.
  • The paint tube was invented in 1841. Once watercolors were sold in tubes, a tube of Chinese white was added to paintboxes, so any watercolor could be made opaque.
  • Later in the 19th century, cheap gouache-like poster paint with a dextrin binder was mass-produced for decorating. In the 20th century, manufacturers began making finer gouache in tubes for artists.

In 19th-century England the mixing of opaque and transparent paint was a point of dispute. Wikipedia's article on watercolor describes rivalries between painters who held to "true", transparent watercolor and early adopters of denser body color.

Famous gouache paintings and artists

On the Tate page for gouache, the works chosen from its collection include J. M. W. Turner's Petworth House: A Bedroom, with a Lady in a Black Silk Dress Seated on a Pink Sofa (1827) and Marc Chagall's The Green Donkey (1911). Wikipedia notes that gouache suits direct, quick painting, including painting outdoors, and names Turner's work as an example.

The best-known modern use is Henri Matisse's. Late in life, after surgery, he worked by painting sheets of paper with gouache and cutting them out. Blue Nude II (1952) is made of gouache-painted paper cut-outs stuck to paper mounted on canvas, and is held by the Musée National d'Art Moderne in Paris. The Museum of Modern Art in New York showed the series in Henri Matisse: The Cut-Outs, from October 2014 to February 2015. Gouache in animation

This is the part most guides to the paint leave out. Wikipedia says most 20th-century animations used gouache to make an opaque color on a cel, with watercolor used for the backgrounds.

The process, as Wikipedia's article on traditional animation describes it, goes like this. Each drawing is inked or photocopied onto a clear sheet of plastic, the cel. Gouache, acrylic or a similar paint is applied to the back of the cel to fill in the colors. Because the cel is clear, several cels can be stacked over one painted background and photographed together. The backgrounds themselves are generally painted in gouache or acrylic, though some productions used watercolor or oil.

So when an old hand-drawn cartoon shows flat, evenly colored characters over softer, more painterly scenery, you may be looking at two kinds of paint at once. Our guide to types of animation explains where cel animation fits, and what 2D animation is covers how it changed once computers took over the coloring.

In Asia, a cousin of gouache called poster color is common. Wikipedia describes Asian poster colors as similar to gouache but thinner and cheaper, and lists animation production among their uses.

How to recognize gouache on a gallery wall

Labels help, but you can often tell before you read one:

  • Look for paper that does not shine through. In a watercolor the white of the paper is the light; in gouache the white is paint.
  • Look for pale strokes laid over dark ones, such as a white highlight on a dark coat. That is easy in gouache and awkward in watercolor.
  • Look at the surface. Gouache dries matte, so it does not shine under the gallery lights.
  • Read the medium line. "Watercolor and gouache" or "bodycolor" is common, because artists often mixed the two, along with ink or pencil, especially in the 19th century.

Wikipedia says commercial artists use gouache most consistently, for posters, illustration, comics and design. For the design side of visual work see concept art and mood boards. Questions people ask

What is gouache in simple words?

It is watercolor made thick and opaque. The paint has more pigment, more binder and often a white filler such as chalk, so a layer of it covers the paper instead of letting it glow through. It dries fast and flat, with a matte, slightly chalky look.

How do you pronounce gouache?

In English it is said 'goo-AHSH' or 'GWAHSH', rhyming with 'wash'. The word comes from French, which took it from the Italian guazzo.

Is gouache the same as watercolor?

No, but they are close relatives. Both are bound with gum and thinned with water, and both can be rewetted after drying. Gouache has larger pigment particles, a higher ratio of pigment to binder and often a white filler, so it is opaque, while watercolor is transparent.

Is gouache the same as acrylic?

No. Traditional gouache stays water-soluble after drying, so a wet brush can lift it again. Acrylic dries into a water-resistant film. There is a hybrid, acrylic gouache, with an acrylic binder: it is matte and opaque like gouache but water-resistant once dry.

What is gouache used for?

Posters, illustration, comics and design work, according to Wikipedia, because it covers quickly and dries fast. Painters use it outdoors and for highlights on watercolors, and for most of the 20th century animation studios used it to color cels.
