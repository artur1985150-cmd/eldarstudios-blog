A storyboard is a series of simple drawings, arranged in order, that shows how a film, cartoon or other moving-image sequence will look before it is made. Each panel stands for a shot: who is in it, where the camera is, and what happens. Arrows and notes mark movement and sound. The modern storyboard was developed at the Walt Disney studio in the early 1930s, and live-action filmmakers picked it up soon after.

What does a storyboard mean in film and animation?

A storyboard is a visual plan of a film, drawn shot by shot like a comic book of the movie. Wikipedia defines it as simple illustrations or images displayed in sequence to pre-visualize a film, animation, motion graphic or interactive sequence.

The panels are usually drawn in rectangles with the same proportions as the screen the film will be shown on. Fast action scenes might need only black-and-white line drawings; a slow, moody drama might call for color sketches that show the light. Arrows indicate camera moves and character motion, and the margins hold notes on dialogue, sound and timing.

The storyboard is not the script. The script says what happens. The storyboard shows how it will look through the lens.

Who invented the storyboard?

Storyboarding in the form we know today grew up at Disney in the early 1930s, but there is more than one origin story. Here are the claims as the sources give them:

D23's Disney A to Z describes Webb Smith as a story man at the studio from 1931 who is credited with the idea of storyboards, used for planning an animated film instead of a script. He left Disney in 1942.

Earlier filmmakers sketched too. Georges Méliès is known as one of the first to use storyboards and pre-production art to plan his effects. What Disney added was a system: Walt Disney set up a separate story department with specialist storyboard artists, a job distinct from animator.

Why did animation need storyboards first?

Because animation is expensive to throw away. Before storyboards, each animator at an American studio was given a scene and made up gags around a theme, with little regard for how the scene fit the whole story. Pinning the drawings up in order let the team see the film as a story and change it before anyone animated a frame.

Wikipedia puts the cost plainly: a few minutes of screen time in traditional animation can mean months of work for a team of animators. If a finished scene does not work in the final cut, all that labor is lost. Planning on paper first is cheaper.

The idea spread fast. Walter Lantz Productions switched from story sketches to storyboards in early 1935. By 1936 Harman-Ising and Leon Schlesinger Productions (the Warner Bros. cartoon studio) had followed, and by 1937 or 1938 every American animation studio used them.

When did live-action films start using storyboards?

Gone with the Wind (1939) was one of the first live-action films to be storyboarded completely. Producer David O. Selznick hired production designer William Cameron Menzies to design every shot. Chris Pallant and Steven Price, in their book Storyboarding: A Critical History, argue that the storyboarding of Disney's Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) influenced Selznick's attempt.

Storyboarding became popular in live-action production in the early 1940s and turned into a standard step. On set, it helps because films are rarely shot in story order: a director can glance at the board to remember how a shot is meant to cut together, instead of rereading the script with the cast and crew waiting.

What goes into a storyboard?

A storyboard can be rough or polished, but most panels answer the same questions. If you ever see storyboards in the extras of a film release or an art book, look for these:

  1. The frame: what the camera sees, drawn in the screen's proportions.
  2. The shot size and angle: a wide view of a street, a close-up of a face, a view from below.
  3. Who is in the shot and where they move, often shown with arrows.
  4. Camera movement: pans, zooms, tracking shots, also marked with arrows.
  5. Notes under or beside the panel: dialogue, sound effects, timing, transitions.

Pallant and Price note that the size, format and materials of storyboards have never been fixed, and that individual panels can be rearranged, removed or added as production goes on. That flexibility is the point.

What is the difference between a storyboard, an animatic and concept art?

These three often get mixed up. A storyboard plans the sequence of shots. An animatic turns the storyboard into a rough moving version. Concept art designs the look of the world.

Storyboard Animatic Concept art
What it is Still panels in order Storyboard panels timed to rough sound Paintings and sketches of settings, characters, costumes
What it answers What we see, from where, in what order How long each shot lasts and how the scene flows What the world looks like
Movement Shown by arrows Simple pans and zooms, cuts in time Usually none

Wikipedia describes the animatic as a sequence of still images, usually taken from the storyboard, played in sync with rough dialogue (scratch vocals) or a rough soundtrack. Directors use it to fix screenplay, camera, shot-list and timing problems before animation starts. According to Pallant and Price, storyboards deal with narrative, editing, camera angles, lenses and camera movement, while concept art handles the design of sets and costumes. Where else are storyboards used?

Outside cinema the same method plans many kinds of sequence. Advertising agencies make presentation boards, more polished than shooting boards, to pitch commercials, and photomatics, which are still photos edited together with sound to test an idea. Software designers storyboard the screens a user will see. Some novelists use them to plot scenes.

Comics have their own version. In Japanese manga, a rough storyboard of the pages is called a "name" (nēmu). Comic writers such as Carl Barks and John Stanley wrote scripts as rough storyboard sketches with notes for the artist. More on Japanese comics is in our guide to what manga is.

Storyboards come before every technique, from hand drawing to stop motion, and a board is the first place to judge staging, one of the 12 principles of animation. The overview of types of animation shows what happens after the board is approved, and more is in the cartoons section.

Questions people ask

What is the purpose of a storyboard?

To see a film before it is made. A storyboard shows what each shot contains, from which angle and in what order, so the director and crew can find problems early, estimate cost and save time. In animation it also stops a studio from animating scenes that would later be cut.

Who invented the storyboard?

Disney credited story man Webb Smith with the idea of drawing scenes on separate sheets and pinning them up in sequence. Disney's official encyclopedia, Disney A to Z, also credits him. A competing account, reported by Pete Docter and Don Peri, says Dick Huemer credited Ted Sears, who first proposed it at Fleischer Studios.

What was the first film with a storyboard?

According to Diane Disney Miller's biography of her father, the first complete storyboards were made for the Disney short Three Little Pigs (1933). Gone with the Wind (1939) was one of the first live-action films to be completely storyboarded.

What is the difference between a storyboard and an animatic?

A storyboard is a set of still drawings. An animatic puts those drawings on a timeline with rough dialogue or music, so the team can judge timing and pacing before animation starts.
