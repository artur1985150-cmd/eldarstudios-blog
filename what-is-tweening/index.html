In short

  • Tweening, short for inbetweening, means creating the frames between two key poses so that one image turns smoothly into the next.

Tweening, short for inbetweening, is the part of animation that fills in the frames between two key poses. The animator decides where a movement starts and where it ends; the drawings in between, called in-betweens, carry the image from one pose to the other so the eye sees smooth motion. In hand-drawn studios an assistant drew them. In animation software the computer usually calculates them.

What does tweening mean in animation?

A movement in animation is built from two kinds of frames. Keyframes are the important poses: a character crouching, the top of the jump, the landing. The frames between them are filled in, and that filling is tweening.

Wikipedia's article on key frames notes that two or three key frames over a second do not create the illusion of movement on their own, so the gaps get filled.

Figma's guide puts the minimum plainly: a tween needs at least two keyframes, a start and an end. According to Wikipedia, the positions of the key frames on the timeline set the timing of the movement.

The long word, inbetweening, is the studio term. The short word took over when software arrived, and in a program the finished run of calculated frames is simply called a tween.

Who drew the in-betweens in old animation studios?

In traditional animation the work was split by rank. A lead animator drew the key poses, assistants cleaned them up, and an inbetweener drew everything that was left, usually on a light table so the drawings underneath showed through.

In large studios the assistants also added breakdowns, extra drawings that define the movement in more detail, after a rough version had been tested and approved.

Wikipedia credits Dick Huemer with developing this system in the 1920s and says Art Davis is said to have been the first inbetweener, citing Richard Williams's book The Animator's Survival Kit. The split caught on because it was efficient: the most experienced artist spent time on the poses that mattered, and the many in-between drawings went to junior staff. In small teams the animator still does all of it.

How many in-betweens does a second of animation need?

Film runs at 24 frames per second, but that does not mean 24 drawings. Wikipedia explains the terms animators use:

  • On ones: a new drawing for every frame, 24 a second. Needed for very fast movement, and the usual rate for computer animation.
  • On twos: each drawing held for two frames, 12 a second. Enough for most movement.
  • On threes or fours: slow movements.

Drop to about four drawings a second and the animation can stop reading as movement at all. Twos are old: Émile Cohl's Fantasmagorie (1908), widely considered the first fully animated film, was made that way. Different parts of one shot can even run at different rates. A character may move on twos while the background pans on ones to keep the camera move smooth. The frame rate guide goes further into why 24 became the number.

How does tweening work in software?

In a program the animator sets the keyframes and the software works out the frames between them by interpolation, calculating each in-between value from the two ends.

Figma's example is an object at x = 0 on frame 1 and x = 100 on frame 10: the program places it at every point on the way. The same trick works on almost any property, not only position.

No Film School also lists classic tweening, which is simply the old hand-drawn method under a new name.

The second half of the job is easing. Wikipedia describes ease-in as a move that starts slowly and speeds up, and ease-out as the reverse. Figma warns that a linear tween with no easing looks robotic, because real objects rarely start and stop at full speed. It is the same idea as "slow in and slow out" in the 12 principles of animation.

Wikipedia names Adobe Flash and Animo, from Cambridge Animation Systems, as early programs with automatic interpolation in the late 1990s. The free program Synfig specializes in it.

Where did computer tweening come from?

It came from Canada, and it started from the inbetweener's job. In 1969, according to the National Research Council of Canada's own account, NRC scientist Nestor Burtnyk came back from a conference where a Disney animator had explained how cartoons were made.

A head animator draws the key pictures and assistants draw the fill-in pictures between them. That assistant's work looked like the perfect task for a computer.

Within a year the NRC had a "key frame animation" package that produced sequences from key frames alone. Wikipedia credits Burtnyk and physicist Marceli Wein with programming it. The National Film Board in Montreal was contacted so that artists could experiment with computer animation, and Peter Foldes made the experimental short Metadata (1971) with it.

His next film, Hunger (1974), took a year and a half. The National Film Board describes its rapidly dissolving, ever-evolving images, which follow one man's slide into greed and gluttony. The line drawings dissolve and reshape into new images, using the software's line interpolation. Hunger won a Special Jury Award for short films at Cannes in 1974 and was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film. In 1997 Burtnyk and Wein received an Academy Award for Technical Achievement for their pioneering work on computer-assisted keyframing.

How can you spot tweening on screen?

Every animated film has in-betweens, but how many and how they are made shows if you know where to look.

Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse switched on purpose between 24 drawings a second and 12. Wikipedia notes one scene where Miles and Peter Parker swing through a forest: Miles is animated at 12 frames to show his inexperience, Peter at 24 for smoother movement. The producers called the effect "crunchy".

The opposite look comes from too much automatic tweening. Wikipedia's article on Flash animation lists auto-tweened character movements and jerky walk cycles among the signs of a poorly made Flash cartoon. When a limb glides from pose to pose in a perfectly even line, with no extra drawings to give it weight, you are probably watching a computer fill the gap with nobody adjusting it.

A quick check while watching:

  1. Watch a slow move. If the drawing seems to stutter, it is probably on twos or threes.
  2. Look at starts and stops. Movement that hits full speed at once has little or no easing.

Tweening, morphing and other related terms

Morphing overlaps with tweening: it is a shape tween between two different images, and Figma treats it as one kind of tweening.

Questions people ask

What does tweening mean in simple words?

It means filling the gap between two pictures. An animator sets where something starts and where it ends, and the frames in between are drawn by an assistant or calculated by a computer, so the change looks like smooth movement instead of a jump.

Is tweening the same as inbetweening?

Yes. Tweening is the short form of inbetweening. The long word comes from hand-drawn studios, where the in-between drawings were the job of an inbetweener. In software the short form is more common, and the finished run of frames is called a tween.

What is the difference between keyframes and tweening?

Keyframes are the poses the animator sets directly, such as the start and end of a jump. Tweening is everything that happens between them. A tween needs at least two keyframes, and their positions on the timeline set the timing.

Who invented computer tweening?

No single person, but early key-frame software came from Nestor Burtnyk and Marceli Wein at the National Research Council of Canada around 1970. Peter Foldes used it for Metadata (1971) and Hunger (1974), and the two scientists received a Technical Achievement Academy Award in 1997.

Why do some cartoons look choppier than others?

Usually because they show fewer different drawings each second. Animating on ones gives 24 drawings a second, on twos 12, and slow movements can go to threes or fours. Fewer in-betweens means less smooth motion, which some films now choose on purpose.
