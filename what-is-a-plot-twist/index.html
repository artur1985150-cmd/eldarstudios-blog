In short

  • A plot twist is a sudden change in the direction of a story; at the very end it is called a twist ending or surprise ending.
  • Good twists are prepared with clues, so on a second look they seem fair.
  • Common types: the hidden identity, the unreliable narrator, the false protagonist, the red herring and the reversal of fortune.
  • In 2011 UC San Diego researchers found readers liked twist stories slightly more when the ending had been spoiled in advance.

A plot twist is a sudden change in the direction of a story that the audience did not see coming. It often reveals something hidden, such as who a character really is, and makes you see earlier scenes differently. When it comes at the very end, it is called a twist ending or surprise ending. A fair twist is prepared with clues, so it surprises you the first time and makes sense the second.

What is a plot twist in a story?

It is a turn in the plot that changes where the story is heading or what it meant all along. Some twists add a new conflict. The stronger ones rewrite the past: a scene you took one way turns out to mean the opposite, and the whole story clicks into a new shape.

Writers get there in two main ways. They withhold information, or they mislead with clues that point the wrong way. Neither is cheating as long as the truth was there to find. LiteraryDevices puts the goal in four words: "surprising yet inevitable in retrospect."

Not every story needs a twist. Some have several small ones, and a few are remembered for one big reveal above everything else.

What are the main types of plot twists?

Most twists fall into a handful of patterns that critics have named, some of them going back to Greek drama. The table below lists the common ones with a classic example of each. A warning first: the examples name endings, so skip the right-hand column if you haven't seen these films.

Type What happens Classic example
Anagnorisis (recognition) A character discovers who they or someone else really is Oedipus Rex: Oedipus learns he killed his father and married his mother
Unreliable narrator The person telling the story turns out to have lied or misunderstood The Usual Suspects; Agatha Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd
False protagonist The apparent hero is killed or dropped early Psycho (1960): Marion Crane dies about halfway through
Red herring A false clue sends you after the wrong answer Agatha Christie's And Then There Were None
Peripeteia (reversal) Fortune turns suddenly, for better or worse, but logically The Twilight Zone, "Time Enough at Last": the broken glasses
Flashback A scene from the past explains everything Alfred Hitchcock's Marnie
Reverse or broken chronology Events are shown out of order, so the cause comes last Memento; Harold Pinter's play Betrayal

The oldest examples are very old. Wikipedia points to "The Three Apples" from the Arabian Nights, a murder story with several turns that ends by revealing the culprit as the investigator's own slave.

What is a plot twist in a movie? Famous examples

In film the twist often sits in the last minutes and sends people back to watch again. Psycho kills Marion Crane, played by Janet Leigh, at about the halfway point. Scream does the same to Drew Barrymore's character in the first fifteen minutes. The Usual Suspects hangs everything on one narrator.

Television built whole series on the idea. The Twilight Zone and Tales of the Unexpected ended episode after episode on a reversal. Long-running dramas use bigger, slower ones: Lost spent its final season on a second timeline that turned out, near the end, to be something else entirely.

Twists also travel well into short formats. A web series with five-minute episodes has little room for slow reveals and can end each part on a turn; see what a web series is for how that format works.

Plot twist vs cliffhanger: what's the difference?

A twist reveals something; a cliffhanger stops before something is resolved. The cliffhanger leaves a character in danger or facing a shock at the end of an episode, so the audience returns. A twist can happen anywhere and changes your understanding of the story.

One scene can be both. In The Empire Strikes Back (1980), Luke Skywalker had been told that Darth Vader killed his father. Vader tells him he is his father. That is a recognition twist, and since the answer to it waited for the next film, it worked as a cliffhanger too.

Series with a fixed end have a different problem: their big reveal has to land in the final episode. Our guide to what a limited series is covers that format.

What makes a plot twist good or bad?

A good twist is fair. The clues were in plain sight, the reveal follows from the story's own logic, and on a rewatch it looks obvious. A bad twist comes from nowhere, contradicts what we saw, or rescues the hero with luck the story never set up.

That last failure has an old name: deus ex machina, Latin for "god from the machine." In ancient Greek theater a god was brought on stage by a crane to settle a plot that seemed impossible to resolve. Today the term is used as criticism of any unprepared rescue. Wikipedia's example is a favorite trick of Victorian novelists, the conveniently found lost will.

The unreliable narrator can also go too far. When Agatha Christie used it in The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, critics argued that tricking the reader in that way was unfair, and the novel caused a great deal of controversy.

Foreshadowing is the fix. If a twist is hinted at early, the audience can accept it even when it hurts.

Do spoilers ruin a plot twist?

Probably less than you fear. In 2011, UC San Diego psychologists Jonathan Leavitt and Nicholas Christenfeld gave readers 12 short stories, including ironic-twist stories and mysteries, in spoiled and unspoiled versions. Across three experiments, readers liked the spoiled versions slightly better, even for the twist stories.

The study, published in Psychological Science, had at least 30 readers for each version of each story and left out anyone who had read a story before. The spoiler only helped when it came as a separate paragraph before the story. Worked into the text, it did not.

Leavitt's explanation was that knowing the outcome makes a story easier to process. Christenfeld went further and called plots "just excuses for great writing." That is one study of short fiction, not films, so take it as a hint rather than a rule. Still, it may explain why people happily rewatch twist movies.

Where do plot twists show up besides films?

Everywhere stories are told. Red herrings belong to detective novels and mysteries, recognition scenes go back to Greek tragedy, and the Arabian Nights already had a murder story with several turns. Short horror fiction written for the internet is another place to look for sudden endings; read what creepypasta is.

Some premises are a twist waiting to happen. A character who learns the exact date of their death, for instance, sets up a reveal about whether fate can be dodged. Questions people ask

What is a plot twist in simple words?

It is the moment a story turns in a direction you did not expect, often by revealing something hidden. The best ones also change how you see everything that came before, so you want to go back and look for the clues you missed.

What is the difference between a plot twist and a cliffhanger?

A twist is a reveal: it tells you something that changes the story. A cliffhanger is a pause: it ends an episode with a character in danger or facing a shock, so you come back next time. One scene can do both, as in The Empire Strikes Back.

Is a deus ex machina a plot twist?

It is a twist that most critics count as a failure. The term means "god from the machine" and describes an improbable rescue that the story never prepared. A fair twist follows from what was already on screen or on the page.

Do spoilers ruin plot twists?

Less than people think. In three experiments at UC San Diego, readers of twist, mystery and literary short stories liked the spoiled versions slightly more. The effect only held when the spoiler came before the story, not when it was written into it.
