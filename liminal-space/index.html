A liminal space is a place of passage or waiting: a hallway, a stairwell, an airport gate, a parking lot at night. The word comes from the Latin limen, "threshold." It began as an anthropology term for the in-between stage of a ritual. Since 2019 it has also named an internet aesthetic of empty, familiar places that feel eerie, sad or oddly comforting, with the Backrooms as its best-known example.

What does liminal space mean?

Liminal space means a place or moment between two states, when you have left one thing and not yet arrived at the next. Physically, that is a corridor, a lobby or a road. In everyday talk, it can also mean a period of life, such as the weeks between leaving one job and starting another.

The online meaning is wider than the dictionary one. Wikipedia's article on the aesthetic notes that many popular images show places that are not literally transitional at all: a classroom, a swimming pool, a playground. What they share is that the people, noise or context you expect are missing. A school is a school. A school at midnight, lit and silent, is a liminal space in the internet sense.

Where did the idea of liminality come from?

The idea came from studies of ritual. The folklorist Arnold van Gennep described it in his book Les rites de passage (1909). He split rites of passage into three phases: separation, a liminal phase, and incorporation into a new status.

In the middle phase, a person has lost the old role but not yet gained the new one: no longer a child, not yet an adult. The anthropologist Victor Turner later took up the concept and described people in this phase as "betwixt and between" recognized social positions. Over time the word spread to describe political and cultural change, and then to places.

What is the liminal space aesthetic?

The liminal space aesthetic is a style of internet images showing built places, usually empty, that look familiar but slightly wrong. Typical subjects are stairwells, corridors, hotels, waiting rooms, shopping malls and pools, often with flat artificial light and no one in sight.

Viewers describe the pictures as eerie, surreal, nostalgic or sad, and sometimes as soothing. Writer Peter Heft, drawing on the critic Mark Fisher, called the unexpected emptiness of a schoolhouse a "failure of presence." In a 2022 study, psychologists Alexander Diel and Michael Lewis found that buildings that depart from familiar structural patterns can cause an uncanny-valley reaction, the same discomfort people feel at faces that are almost but not quite human.

The images spread across platforms in 2019. Interest jumped in March 2020, when lockdowns emptied real streets, stations and offices and the internet's favorite strange pictures suddenly looked like the news. The aesthetic sits close to other internet styles in our aesthetics section, such as vaporwave and the Y2K aesthetic, which also play with nostalgia for half-remembered places.

What are the Backrooms?

The Backrooms are a fictional endless maze of empty office rooms with yellow walls, damp carpet and humming fluorescent lights. The idea began on 4chan in May 2019 and is the most famous example of both the liminal space aesthetic and the creepypasta genre of internet horror stories.

On 12 May 2019, an anonymous user on 4chan's paranormal board asked for "disquieting images that just feel off" and posted a photo of a vacant, yellow-lit room. Another user replied with the first description: if you "noclip" out of reality in the wrong place, you end up in the Backrooms, an immense space of empty rooms. Fans then added "levels" and hostile "entities."

The photo itself is real. According to Wikipedia, it was taken in 2002 at a former furniture store in Oshkosh, Wisconsin, during renovation, and its source was only identified in 2024.

How did the Backrooms become a film?

The Backrooms reached a mass audience through Kane Parsons, a young YouTuber who posted a short found-footage film in early 2022. It grew into a web series. ArchDaily, writing in February 2023, described it as a seemingly infinite labyrinth of yellow-tinted office spaces in the style of a 1996 VHS recording.

Parsons built his scenes in Blender and used the worn VHS look partly to hide the simplicity of the 3D sets, ArchDaily notes. In February 2023 a feature adaptation was announced with A24, and Parsons directed it himself. Backrooms premiered in Santa Monica on 7 May 2026 and opened in US theaters on 29 May 2026.

The feature borrows the classic Backrooms setting but adds its own human story, about a struggling furniture store owner who finds a glowing gap in a wall. Parsons has named the work of David Lynch as an early influence on his look.

Which films and series use liminal spaces?

Filmmakers used liminal spaces long before the internet named them. Stanley Kubrick's The Shining (1980) and David Lynch's Twin Peaks (1990) are the usual early examples; Skinamarink, Severance, Exit 8 and Backrooms are the recent ones.

  1. The Shining (1980). Kubrick designed the Overlook Hotel to be physically illogical, with dead-end corridors and overlapping rooms that make a floor plan impossible, and often shot it in cold, close symmetry.
  2. Twin Peaks (1990). David Lynch's Black Lodge, a room of red curtains and zigzag floor, is often cited as an early liminal space on screen.
  3. Inland Empire (2006). Lynch shot this experimental film entirely in standard-definition digital, and its low-fidelity look links it to later internet horror.
  4. Skinamarink (2022). Director Kyle Edward Ball said he looked at online communities devoted to liminal spaces while developing this low-budget horror film.
  5. Severance (2022). Creator Dan Erickson named the Backrooms among the influences on the show.
  6. Exit 8 (2025). Genki Kawamura's film, based on the 2023 video game The Exit 8, has been discussed as liminal-space horror.
  7. Backrooms (2026). Kane Parsons's A24 feature, based on his web series.

Why are liminal spaces so popular?

People share liminal space images because they produce two feelings at once: recognition and unease. Commentators link the appeal to nostalgia; the images give few details, so viewers fill them with their own memories of a school, a hotel or a mall from childhood.

There is also a simple practical reason. Anyone can take such a photo. An empty stairwell or a parking garage at 3 a.m. needs no special equipment, which made the aesthetic easy to join and easy to spread. And because the Backrooms had no single author or official canon, every new photo, story and video could add to it, the same way older creepypastas grew.

Questions people ask

What is a liminal space in simple terms?

It is a place you normally pass through rather than stay in, like a hallway, stairwell, waiting room or airport gate. Online, the term also covers any familiar place shown empty and out of context, such as a school at night, which can feel strangely calm or unsettling.

Why do liminal spaces feel creepy?

They show places built for people without any people in them. Writer Peter Heft linked this to a "failure of presence": something expected is missing. A 2022 study by Alexander Diel and Michael Lewis found that buildings that break familiar patterns can trigger an uncanny-valley reaction.

What is an example of a liminal space?

Common examples are an empty hotel corridor, a stairwell, a shopping mall after closing, a waiting room, a long road at night and an empty classroom. The most famous imaginary one is the Backrooms: endless yellow rooms with old carpet and buzzing fluorescent lights.

Are the Backrooms real?

No. The Backrooms is a fictional place invented in a 4chan thread in May 2019. The photo that started it is real: Wikipedia reports it was taken in 2002 at a former furniture store in Oshkosh, Wisconsin, during renovation, and its origin was found in 2024.

Where does the word liminal come from?

From the Latin limen, which means threshold. The anthropologist Arnold van Gennep used the idea in his 1909 book Les rites de passage for the middle stage of a ritual, when a person has left an old status but has not yet entered a new one.
