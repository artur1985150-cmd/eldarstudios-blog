In short

  • A Dutch angle (also Dutch tilt or canted angle) is a shot in which the camera is rotated sideways, so the horizon and vertical lines sit at a slant.
  • Its main job is to make the viewer feel that something is wrong: madness, tension, danger or disorientation.
  • The name is usually explained as a corruption of 'Deutsch' (German), but that theory is not proven.
  • The earliest recorded example is Dream of a Rarebit Fiend (1906); German Expressionist films of the 1920s used it widely.
  • The Third Man (1949) uses tilted shots to stress its hero's alienation, and Batman (1966) tilted the camera in villains' lairs because the crooks were crooked.

A Dutch angle is a shot in which the camera is tilted sideways, so the horizon and the vertical lines in the picture sit at a slant instead of running straight. It looks like the view when you tip your head toward one shoulder. Filmmakers use it to make a scene feel uneasy. It is also called a Dutch tilt, a canted angle or an oblique angle.

What does Dutch angle mean?

A Dutch angle is a camera set at a slant. Wikipedia describes it as a shot composed with vertical lines at an angle to the side of the frame, with the horizon often off level too.

The other names say the same thing in different words. Canted angle, oblique angle and Dutch tilt are the common ones; Wikipedia also lists "vortex plane" and "Durkin". FilmDaft notes there is no fixed number of degrees that turns a shot into a Dutch angle, and viewers notice even a slight tilt.

The camera can also move while staying tilted: Wikipedia says it can pivot, pan or track along the diagonal axis.

Why is it called a Dutch angle?

The honest answer is that nobody is sure. The most repeated theory is that "Dutch" is a corruption of the German word Deutsch, because silent-era German films used the shot so much. It sounds neat, but FilmDaft points out that it has never been proven.

Wikipedia gives a second explanation. Wikipedia points out that "Dutch" can mark something out of the ordinary, as in Dutch uncle, Dutch treat or Dutch auction, and that the aeronautics term Dutch roll means something out of line. A tilted frame is out of line, so the word may simply describe it. Either way, it has nothing to do with the Netherlands.

What is a Dutch angle used for?

The main job of a Dutch angle is to make the viewer uneasy. Adobe says it gives a sense that something is off with a character or within the scene.

Adobe's guide lists the genres where it turns up most: thrillers and psychological dramas, horror, comedy for surreal effect, and science fiction for unfamiliar worlds. Common reasons to tilt include:

  • Madness or a character's unstable mind.
  • Drug psychosis, as Wikipedia says of Terry Gilliam's films.
  • The inner terror of characters in horror, in Adobe's words.
  • Villains who are morally crooked.
  • Disorientation in a strange or unfamiliar place.

FilmDaft adds a subtler use: tilting a shot of two people places one higher in the frame, which can show who has the upper hand.

Where did the Dutch angle come from?

The tilt is older than the German films it is named after. According to Wikipedia, the earliest recorded example is Edwin S. Porter's Dream of a Rarebit Fiend (1906), based on Winsor McCay's comic strip, which already used oblique angles in its drawings.

German Expressionist films widely used it to depict madness, unrest, exoticism and disorientation. Wikipedia adds that montages of Dutch angles are often built from opposed tilts, a frame leaning right followed by one leaning left. Dziga Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (1929) contains Dutch angles among its other innovations.

Wikipedia notes that Alfred Hitchcock spent time at the UFA studios in Germany early in his career, and lists tilted shots in his Suspicion (1941), Notorious (1946), Strangers on a Train (1951) and The Man Who Knew Too Much (1956).

Caligari: the tilted world that was built, not filmed

The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), directed by Robert Wiene, is the film most often named when people talk about Dutch angles. Look closely, though, and the camera is mostly level. Wikipedia describes the camerawork as fairly simple, alternating medium shots and straight-on angles to show off the sets.

The slant is in the sets themselves. Wikipedia describes structures that lean and twist at odd angles, narrow spiralling streets, and shadows and streaks of light painted directly onto the sets. FilmDaft puts it in one line: the slant is built into the sets.

The Third Man and Batman: two famous cases

The Third Man (1949), directed by Carol Reed and shot by Robert Krasker, is the classic example. Wikipedia says its harsh lighting and Dutch angles, combined with the ruins of postwar Vienna, evoke exhaustion and cynicism at the start of the Cold War. Elsewhere it adds that the tilts emphasize the main character's alienation in a foreign environment.

Not everyone approved. The British critic C. A. Lejeune called Reed's tilted framing "most distracting". The director William Wyler sent Reed a spirit level with a note suggesting he put it on top of the camera next time. Krasker still won the Academy Award for black-and-white cinematography, as FilmDaft notes.

The 1960s Batman TV series used the tilt extensively. Its cameras were deliberately set out of level, most often on the sets of villains' lairs. Wikipedia explains the reason: it gave the scenes a surreal, comic-book quality and suggested that the crooks were crooked.

Modern examples, and when it goes wrong

Wikipedia says Dutch angles are frequently used by directors with a background in the visual arts. It names Tim Burton in Edward Scissorhands and Ed Wood, and Terry Gilliam in Brazil, The Fisher King, 12 Monkeys and Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas.

Sam Raimi used it in the Evil Dead films to show that a character had become possessed by evil. Tom Hooper frequently uses it, as in The King's Speech (2010) and Les Misérables (2012).

The shot also appears in video games. Wikipedia says it is often used in horror games with static camera angles, citing early Resident Evil and Silent Hill titles.

Because it is so visible, it is easy to overdo. Battlefield Earth (2000) drew sharp criticism for its pervasive use of the shot. Wikipedia quotes Roger Ebert saying that its director had learned from better films that "directors sometimes tilt their cameras", but never learned why. FilmDaft notes that a tilted frame stands out because most shots are level.

How to spot a Dutch angle while you watch

Look for the lines that should be straight: door frames, the horizon, the edge of a table, a building. If they all lean the same way while the actors stand normally, the camera is tilted.

Then ask why it tilted at that moment. Usually the answer is in the story: a lie, a threat, a mind slipping. A sudden switch from level shots to tilted ones, then back again, is the director telling you the scene has turned.

Questions people ask

What is a Dutch angle in simple words?

It is a shot where the camera is tilted to one side, so the floor, the horizon and the walls look slanted. It feels like tilting your head. Directors use it when they want a scene to feel off balance, tense or slightly mad.

Why is it called a Dutch angle?

Nobody knows for sure. The common explanation is that 'Dutch' is a corruption of 'Deutsch', German, because German silent films used the shot so much. Another theory is that 'Dutch' here means out of the ordinary or out of line, as in the aeronautics term Dutch roll.

What is the difference between a Dutch angle and a tilt shot?

A tilt shot moves the camera up or down, like nodding your head, to show something above or below. A Dutch angle tips the camera sideways, like tipping your head toward your shoulder. Adobe's guide makes the same distinction: a basic tilt reveals information, a Dutch angle changes the feel.

What movies use Dutch angles?

Well-known examples include The Third Man (1949), Hitchcock's Suspicion (1941) and Strangers on a Train (1951), the Batman TV series and film of the 1960s, Terry Gilliam's Brazil and 12 Monkeys, Sam Raimi's Evil Dead films and Tom Hooper's The King's Speech (2010).

Can a Dutch angle be overused?

Yes. Wikipedia says Battlefield Earth (2000) drew sharp criticism for its pervasive use of the shot; Roger Ebert wrote that its director had learned that directors sometimes tilt their cameras, but never learned why. FilmDaft adds that a tilted frame stands out because most shots are level.
