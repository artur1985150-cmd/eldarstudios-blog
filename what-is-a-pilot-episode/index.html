In short

  • A pilot is a trial episode made to convince a network or streamer to order a whole series.
  • Variety estimates that the big four US networks review about 100 pilots a year, and only a little over a quarter become series.
  • A premise pilot starts the story; a proof-of-concept pilot shows what a typical episode looks like.
  • Pilots often differ from the series that follows: roles get recast, scenes reshot, music rewritten.
  • Some shows skip the pilot and go straight to series, a model that spread after Netflix popularized it.

A pilot episode is a trial episode of a planned TV series, made to sell the show to a broadcaster or another distributor. If the buyers like it, they order the series, and the pilot often airs as the first episode. If they don't, it may never be shown at all. That is why many pilots look a little different from the rest of the series: they were made before the show existed.

What does pilot episode mean?

Wikipedia defines a television pilot as a standalone episode used to sell a show to a broadcaster or other distributor. It is a test. Networks use it to see whether a concept actually works on screen and whether more episodes are worth the money.

Final Draft calls a pilot "a testing ground, proof of concept, and seed" for a whole series. It introduces the core concept, the world and the characters of the show in one episode.

The odds are poor. Variety, as quoted by Wikipedia, estimates that the four big American networks review roughly 100 pilots a year, and only a little over a quarter go on to become series. The rest are never seen by viewers.

Why is it called a pilot?

There is no settled answer. StudioBinder gives three theories: the pilot light that lights a bigger flame, the pilot who steers a ship or plane, and a Greek root meaning prototype. It leans toward the prototype idea.

Wikipedia makes a similar comparison with science: a pilot experiment is a small trial run before a full research project gets time and money. A TV pilot does the same job for a series.

How does a pilot become a series?

On American broadcast TV this has followed a yearly calendar, known as pilot season. Wikipedia describes it this way: each major network hears about 500 short pitches. Before the 2010–11 season, each network asked for scripts for about 70 of them and, months later, ordered about 20 pilots.

Casting is a long, competitive process. For the 1994 pilot of Friends, casting director Ellie Kanner looked at more than 1,000 head shots for each of the six main roles and called back 75 actors to audition again.

The finished pilots go to studio and network executives, and sometimes test audiences. Each network then picks about four to eight for series and presents them to advertisers at the upfronts in May. Even then, only one or two of the new shows typically last beyond a season.

Not every pilot is ordered by a network first. StudioBinder notes that some are made independently as a spec, then sold. It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia started as a cheap spec pilot that FX bought; FX did not air it and had new episodes made with a bigger budget.

Types of TV pilots

Wikipedia lists several kinds. The first two are the ones you are most likely to watch.

Type What it is Example
Premise pilot Introduces the characters and their world; built to air as episode one Gilligan's Island, which opened with the castaways newly stranded
Proof of concept Set further into the story to show a typical episode Lassie's "The Well," which aired well into season one
Backdoor pilot A TV film or miniseries that can air on its own if no series follows A TV movie that can air even if no series is ordered
Put pilot The network has agreed to air it or pay the studio a penalty Usually guarantees a pickup
Unsold pilot Made but never ordered to series Some were later aired in summer anthology shows
Test run A few episodes air as a short series instead of one pilot Used by some talk shows

Final Draft adds a rule of thumb: premise pilots are usually hour-long dramas, while non-premise pilots fall under the sitcom category. Wikipedia notes that Remington Steele and Lassie each had both kinds. For how one show can grow out of another, see what a spin-off is.

Why does the pilot look different from the rest of the show?

Because the show changes between the pilot and the order. Wikipedia's examples show how far it can go.

Gilligan's Island recast three roles after the pilot, so CBS aired the second episode first instead. The original theme song, a calypso number, was rewritten from scratch. Cagney & Lacey began as a TV movie with Loretta Swit as Cagney; when she could not leave her contract, the role went to Meg Foster, and later to Sharon Gless.

Star Trek went through two pilots. The first, "The Cage," did not sell, and Desilu head Lucille Ball persuaded NBC to pay for a second, "Where No Man Has Gone Before." Spock was the only major character in both. Footage from "The Cage" was later reused in the two-part episode "The Menagerie."

Sometimes the pilot is remade outright. StudioBinder notes that a network may reshoot a picked-up pilot with a higher budget or a different cast. So if the first episode of an old show feels off, with a different set or a face you never see again, that can be why.

Pilot vs series premiere

The pilot is the episode that sold the show; the series premiere is the first one that airs. Often they are the same episode, but not always.

Term What it means
Pilot The trial episode shown to buyers
Series premiere The first episode broadcast to viewers
Straight to series A season ordered without any pilot
Backdoor pilot A standalone TV film or miniseries that tests a possible series

Firefly's pilot, "Serenity," was rejected by the network, so a new first episode, "Train Job," was shot for broadcast. The pilot that sold Lost Girl, "Vexed," aired eighth. The Sarah Jane Adventures was ordered before anything was filmed, so strictly it had no pilot, yet its first episode is routinely called one. Wikipedia credits Amazing Stories (1985) as one of the first shows commissioned without a pilot and says the straight-to-series model has grown since Netflix popularized it.

A pilot also differs from a limited series opener: a limited series is planned to end, while a pilot is built to keep going for as long as audiences will watch.

What happens to pilots that don't sell?

Some vanish. Others find a second life. In 1956 American networks began airing packages of unsold pilots in summer anthology series; ABC's G.E. Summer Originals and NBC's Sneak Preview both premiered on 3 July 1956. The last such series, CBS Summer Playhouse, ran from 1987 to 1989.

A few became films. Mulholland Drive (2001) was an unsold pilot with an ending shot specially for the cinema. Twin Peaks is a different case: according to StudioBinder, the original pilot answered the question of who killed Laura Palmer, and that reveal was cut from the broadcast pilot.

Questions people ask

What is a pilot episode in simple words?

It is a test episode. Producers make one episode of a planned show and show it to a TV network or streaming service, which then decides whether to pay for a full season. If the show is ordered, the pilot is often aired as the first episode.

Why is it called a pilot?

Nobody knows for sure. StudioBinder lists three theories: the pilot light that starts a larger flame, the pilot who guides a ship or plane, and a Greek root meaning prototype, which it thinks most likely. Wikipedia compares TV pilots to pilot experiments, the small trials that come before a full research project.

Is the pilot always the first episode?

No. It may air first, later in the season or never. The second Star Trek pilot, Where No Man Has Gone Before, aired as the third episode of season one, and the pilot of Lost Girl aired as episode eight. Firefly's pilot was rejected by the network, and a new first episode was shot.

How long is a pilot script?

Final Draft gives 22 to 35 pages for a half-hour sitcom and 45 to 65 pages for a one-hour drama, with one page equal to roughly one minute of screen time.

Do streaming shows have pilots?

Many do not. A network can order a season straight to series without seeing a finished episode. Wikipedia credits Amazing Stories (1985) as one of the first shows commissioned that way and says the model has grown since Netflix popularized it, though many such shows still title their first episode Pilot.
