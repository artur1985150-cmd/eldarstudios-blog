A creepypasta is a horror story or legend made and shared on the internet. The word mixes "creepy" with "copypasta", slang for text copied and pasted across websites, and it appeared on 4chan around 2007. Most creepypastas are short, user-written tales meant to frighten, often told as if they were true. Today the term covers almost any horror fiction first posted online, from Slender Man to the Backrooms.

What does creepypasta mean?

Creepypasta means a scary story that spreads by being copied around the internet. On 4chan, "copypasta" meant any block of text people pasted from board to board until it went viral; a creepypasta is the horror version. Unlike copypasta, every creepypasta is horror fiction.

A 2014 post on the Library of Congress blog Folklife Today put it neatly: creepypasta is copypasta that is also creepy, and usually a kind of urban legend, a story told as true but of unknown origin. Scholars treat it as modern folklore. Where older legends passed by word of mouth, creepypastas pass through forums, wikis and video sites.

The form has grown beyond plain text. A creepypasta can be a single paragraph or a series running for years, and it can include photos, videos, links and GIFs.

Where did creepypasta come from?

Creepypasta grew out of 4chan in the 2000s, but its roots are older. Wikipedia notes that some writers trace it to the chain emails of the 1990s, which spread hoaxes and urban legends by threatening a terrible fate to anyone who did not forward them.

Some readers count Ted the Caver (2001) as the first creepypasta. It was presented as the online diary of a caver digging into a narrow passage, and message boards argued for years about whether it was real. The author later said it was based on his real caving trips with many invented details added.

From the late 2000s the genre got its own homes. Creepypasta.com started in 2008; the Creepypasta Wiki and Reddit's r/nosleep, a forum for first-person horror stories, both began in 2010. Over time named authors replaced anonymous posters, and in 2014 the Library of Congress's American Folklife Center included creepypasta sites in its web archive of digital culture.

What are the most famous creepypastas?

The best-known creepypastas are Slender Man, Jeff the Killer, Ben Drowned, Candle Cove, Ted the Caver and the Backrooms. Each started on a different site, and each shows a different way the genre works.

Slender Man is the most famous. Eric Knudsen, posting as Victor Surge, created him on the Something Awful forums in June 2009 for a Photoshop contest asking for "paranormal images." Other people kept adding stories, so the character never had a single canon. The web series Marble Hornets (2009 to 2014) and the game Slender: The Eight Pages (2012) spread him further.

Ben Drowned was created by Alexander D. Hall under the name Jadusable. It began in 2010 as the tale of a haunted Nintendo 64 cartridge of The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask and grew into a three-part web series and alternate reality game that ended on 31 October 2020.

Candle Cove, by the web cartoonist Kris Straub, is written as a forum thread where people slowly realize they all remember the same disturbing puppet show. Straub has said an article in The Onion about a man's nightmares of the 1970s show Lidsville gave him the idea.

What are the main types of creepypasta?

Most creepypastas fall into a few repeating types. The most common are lost episodes of TV shows, haunted video games, stories built around one photograph, and diary or forum-thread stories that pretend to be real records.

  1. Lost episodes. A supposed unaired or forgotten episode of a familiar show turns horrifying. Creepypasta.com calls this one of the most popular tropes; Candle Cove is a gentler variation.
  2. Haunted games. A copy of a game hides something malicious that leaks into the player's life, as in Ben Drowned.
  3. Image-led stories. One unsettling picture comes first and the story grows around it, as with Jeff the Killer and the Backrooms.
  4. Diaries and threads. The story is told through dated entries or forum replies, as in Ted the Caver and Candle Cove, so it feels like evidence rather than fiction.

The image-led kind connects to a wider internet style. The Backrooms photo is the most famous liminal space picture, and the two trends grew up together after 2019.

How did creepypastas move to film and TV?

Creepypastas reached the screen first as homemade web series and then as studio projects. Marble Hornets was a YouTube series, then the 2015 film Always Watching. Slender Man got a studio feature in 2018, and the A24 film Backrooms followed in 2026.

Television took the idea further. Each season of the Syfy anthology series Channel Zero was based on a different creepypasta, starting with Candle Cove. The format suits an anthology because every story is short and self-contained; our guide to the web series shows how online-first shows like Marble Hornets grew. The Backrooms took a similar path: Kane Parsons's YouTube series became a feature film he directed himself.

Video games followed too. Slender: The Eight Pages (2012) and its sequel Slender: The Arrival (2013) turned the forum character into a game, and the Slender Man also inspired the Enderman in Minecraft.

Why did Slender Man make the news?

Slender Man made international news because of a real crime in 2014. On 31 May 2014 in Waukesha, Wisconsin, two 12-year-old girls stabbed a 12-year-old friend 19 times, saying they believed they had to do it for the Slender Man. She survived after a passing cyclist found her.

Both girls were tried as adults. In September 2017, as CBS News reported, a jury found Anissa Weier not criminally responsible because she was mentally ill at the time, and she was sent to a mental hospital rather than prison; the second girl, Morgan Geyser, was also committed to a psychiatric facility. After the attack, Creepypasta.com published a statement of sympathy and stressed that its stories are fiction. Some creepypasta sites added reminders about the line between fiction and reality.

The case started a wide debate about children and the internet. Wikipedia describes a wider pattern of young readers taking creepypastas for true stories, because the genre is written to feel real.

How can you tell a creepypasta from a true story?

Assume it is fiction. Creepypastas copy the look of real records on purpose: diary dates, forum usernames, grainy photos, "found" video. Real events leave news reports, court records and named witnesses; a creepypasta usually leaves only copies of itself.

A few questions help. Who first posted it, and where? Does any outside source mention the event? Did the story change as it spread? With Slender Man the answer is clear: a named forum user made him for a contest in 2009. That is part of the appeal of the genre for many readers, a story that pretends to be true while everyone involved knows it is not. Questions people ask

What does creepypasta mean?

It means a scary story copied and shared around the internet. The word joins "creepy" with "copypasta", 4chan slang for any block of text that people copy and paste from site to site until it goes viral.

Are creepypastas real?

No. Creepypastas are fiction, even when they are written as diaries, forum posts or found footage to feel true. Creepypasta.com publicly stated in 2014 that its stories are fictional, after a crime in Wisconsin was linked to the Slender Man character.

What was the first creepypasta?

There is no single agreed answer. Some point to Ted the Caver (2001), an online caving diary that readers debated as real. Others see roots in the chain emails of the 1990s. The word itself only appeared around 2007.

Who created Slender Man?

Eric Knudsen, posting as Victor Surge on the Something Awful forums in June 2009. He made the character for a Photoshop contest that asked users to create paranormal images, showing a very tall, faceless figure in a black suit.

Is creepypasta suitable for kids?

Many creepypastas deal with murder, suicide and violence, and their realistic style can confuse younger readers about what is true. Wikipedia notes a pattern of children mistaking these stories for fact. Parents may want to read a story first.
