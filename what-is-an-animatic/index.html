In short

  • An animatic is a sequence of still images, usually storyboard panels, played in time with rough dialogue or a rough soundtrack.
  • It comes after the storyboard and before animation, so the team can fix story, camera and timing problems while they are cheap.
  • Its predecessor was Disney's Leica reel, which filmed storyboards and cut them to the soundtrack.
  • Dictionary.com dates the word to 1975–80, perhaps a blend of 'animate' and 'automatic'; Wikipedia credits Disney with coining it.
  • Viewers can see animatics in DVD special features and in studio YouTube channels.

An animatic is a storyboard turned into a rough video. The still drawings are played in order, each held on screen as long as its shot will last, over scratch dialogue, temporary music or sound effects. It lets directors and animators test timing, pacing and camera choices before the expensive animation begins. In animation it comes after the storyboard and before production; live-action films use it for complex scenes.

What does animatic mean?

Dictionary.com defines an animatic as a preliminary form of a film, TV commercial or other video, made of a series of drawings with audio and usually assembled in video editing software. Wikipedia's version: a sequence of still images, usually taken from a storyboard, displayed in sync with rough dialogue (scratch vocals) or a rough soundtrack.

Boords, whose founder is James Chambers, calls it simply an animated storyboard. Its parts, as Boords lists them, are frames (rough sketches or stills), a timing for each frame, and sound.

Disney's Leica reel did an animatic's job, and the dictionary's first record of the word falls in the same years that cheap video made animatics a regular tool for advertising.

How is an animatic different from a storyboard?

A storyboard is read; an animatic is watched. A storyboard is a set of still panels you look through at your own pace. The animatic puts the same panels on a timeline, so a two-second shot lasts two seconds and the joke lands, or does not, at the speed the audience will see it.

The animatic uses the same images as the storyboard, sequenced and rendered as video. Wikipedia calls it a video of a hand-drawn storyboard with very limited added motion, set to a soundtrack. Boords gives each frame a duration that matches its shot, with scratch audio such as voiceover, music or sound effects.

StudioBinder calls a storyboard a rough approximation of how a sequence will unfold. An animatic adds sound and timing to show how the sequence flows in motion.

Why do animators make animatics?

To find mistakes while they are cheap. Wikipedia explains that the animatic lets animators and directors work out screenplay, camera position, shot list and timing problems in the storyboard. The board and soundtrack are revised, a new animatic is cut and reviewed, and the loop repeats until the storyboard is final.

The reason is cost. A few minutes of screen time in traditional animation usually means months of work for a team of animators, Wikipedia notes, and a finished scene that does not work in the final cut is labor written off. In computer animation the same planning avoids building models and sets that will never appear in frame.

Where does the animatic fit in production?

In hand-drawn animation, Wikipedia places it after the soundtrack is recorded and before full animation. A timing director then reads the animatic to work out which poses, drawings and mouth shapes each frame needs.

The animatic stays in use until the end. As finished scenes are approved, they are spliced into the animatic or Leica reel in place of the pencil animation. Live action works the same way: on Star Wars: Episode I, finished dailies replaced sections of the animatic as shooting progressed.

Do live-action films use animatics?

Yes, to plan complex shots. In feature films the computer-made version is usually called previsualization, or previs. Wikipedia describes previs as rough computer graphics, sometimes just grey shapes, edited with temporary music and dialogue to plan complex shots.

A few cases from Wikipedia's history of previs:

  • Francis Ford Coppola built One from the Heart (1982) on an animatic: actors recorded the script radio-style and storyboard artists drew more than 1,800 frames for the animatic.
  • For Mission: Impossible (1996), David Dozoretz and John Knoll made digital animatics for the final chase.
  • On Star Wars: Episode I, Dozoretz's team made an average of four to six animatics for every effects shot.

Ad agencies still use animatics for cheap test commercials. A variant, the "rip-o-matic", is made from scenes of existing movies, television programs or commercials.

Where can you watch an animatic?

In the extras. Wikipedia notes that some feature film DVD special features include production animatics, with scratch voices or sometimes the real cast. These are usually scenes that were cut after the voices were recorded but before animation.

Studios also post animatics online. StudioBinder embeds a SpongeBob animatic and points to the Nick Animation YouTube channel, which has more such videos; you can see how much of the comic timing is fixed before the full animation begins. Questions people ask

What is the difference between a storyboard and an animatic?

A storyboard is a set of still drawings on paper or screen, read panel by panel. An animatic plays those drawings as a video, each held as long as its shot will last, with rough sound. The storyboard shows what happens; the animatic shows how long it takes and how it flows.

Is an animatic the same as an animation?

No. An animatic is a planning tool built from rough still images with very limited added motion. The animation is the finished work. Scenes from the animatic are replaced by finished animation as production goes on.

Why are animatics important?

Because animation is expensive to throw away. A few minutes of hand-drawn animation can take a team months, and a scene cut after it is finished wastes all that work. Cutting the film at the animatic stage shows what to drop before anyone animates it.

What does an animatic include?

Storyboard frames, usually rough sketches or stills, a duration for each frame that matches its shot, and scratch sound such as voiceover, music or sound effects.

Do live-action films use animatics?

Yes, to plan complex shots and visual effects. Advertising agencies used them for TV commercials from the 1970s. In feature films the digital version is usually called previsualization, or previs; Star Wars: Episode I used four to six animatics for each effects shot.
